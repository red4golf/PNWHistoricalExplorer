# Rajneeshpuram: The Commune That Took Over a Town

You stand on the Big Muddy Ranch in central Oregon's Wasco County, on land a religious commune renamed Rajneeshpuram in 1981. Nothing marks it as a former city today. Followers of the Indian teacher Bhagwan Shree Rajneesh bought the ranch for $5.75 million that year and built a commune that peaked near 7,000 residents. What they left behind is a record of an election takeover, a mass poisoning, and federal indictments that ended with Rajneesh's arrest in 1985.

## 1981: Buying the Big Muddy Ranch

The Big Muddy Ranch was purchased in 1981 for $5.75 million. The property spanned nearly 70,000 acres across Wasco and Jefferson counties, according to the Oregon Encyclopedia. Rajneesh's followers built a city on the open range there.

## Taking Antelope

The nearest town, Antelope, had roughly fifty residents. The Oregon Encyclopedia describes how the Rajneeshees took political control of it by a simple method: they moved in and outvoted the existing residents in town elections. That gave the commune the town council, the general store, and the school.

## A City That Peaked Near 7,000

The commune's population reached about 7,000 at its height, per the Oregon Encyclopedia, though not all of those residents lived there full time. Bhagwan Rajneesh kept largely silent in public during these years. Ma Anand Sheela became the commune's spokesperson and chief lieutenant during that silence, speaking for Rajneeshpuram in his place.

## The Dalles Attack and a Federal Case

Antelope was not the only vote the commune tried to move. According to the Oregon Encyclopedia, the commune also bused homeless people to the ranch and registered them to vote in local elections. By 1985 federal agents were investigating immigration fraud, vote tampering, illegal wiretapping, and the salad-bar attack in The Dalles all at once.

Followers contaminated salad bars at ten restaurants in The Dalles, Oregon, with salmonella bacteria between September 9 and October 10, 1984. Lab tests later matched the strain to one found in a commune lab. A 1997 study in the Journal of the American Medical Association counted 751 people sickened, 388 confirmed by lab culture and 363 more diagnosed on symptoms alone. At least 45 were hospitalized. No one died. The Oregon Encyclopedia and the JAMA researchers point to the same motive: a local election the commune wanted to sway.

## Arrests, Sentencing, and Collapse

Federal agents intercepted two chartered jets during a refueling stop in Charlotte, North Carolina, around 2 a.m. on October 28, 1985. They took Rajneesh and 12 others into custody on the spot. A 35-count federal indictment charged him with conspiracy to break immigration law, making false statements to the INS, harboring people in the country illegally, and fleeing to avoid prosecution, according to the Washington Post. He was later deported.

Ma Anand Sheela pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit illegal wiretapping and conspiracy to tamper with a consumer product, the charge covering the salad-bar contamination. A federal court sentenced her to four and a half years in custody on July 22, 1986. Her co-defendant, Ma Anand Puja — known outside the commune as Diane Onang — got the same sentence. The record is public in United States v. Sheela, 667 F. Supp. 724 (D. Or. 1987).

Sheela and other commune leaders left the ranch abruptly on September 14, 1985. The community did not survive their departure. Courts later ruled that Rajneeshpuram's 1985 incorporation as a city had been illegal. By 1986, the ranch that had held nearly 7,000 residents stood empty. The property that cost $5.75 million in 1981 had become, within five years, a ghost town.
