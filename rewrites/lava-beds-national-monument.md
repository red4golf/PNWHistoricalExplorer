# Lava Beds National Monument

You stand on a field of broken black rock in the far northeast corner of California, close enough to Oregon to see it. Nothing moves. Trenches, cave mouths, low stone walls, and cinder cones cut across the ground under your boots. This is where somewhere between 50 and 60 Modoc fighters, led by Kintpuash, held off a U.S. Army that numbered nearly 1,000 troops at its peak. The odds ran roughly ten to one.

## Captain Jack's Stronghold

Roughly 150 Modoc men, women, and children sheltered inside a natural lava fortress known as Captain Jack's Stronghold, according to the National Park Service. They held out for about five months, including through winter. Kintpuash — the leader Americans knew as Captain Jack — used the stronghold's trenches and caves as a defensive line. On January 17, 1873, more than 300 U.S. soldiers attacked it. The Modoc repelled them.

## Six Months of War, 1872-1873

The Modoc War began November 29, 1872. Kintpuash led the Modoc band that had resisted removal to the Klamath Reservation.

Peace talks collapsed on April 11, 1873, when Modoc warriors killed General Edward R.S. Canby and the Reverend Eleazar Thomas during a parley called to end the war. On April 26, Modoc fighters under Scarfaced Charley ambushed a 69-soldier Army patrol and killed both of its commanding officers. The clash became known as the Thomas-Wright Massacre. The war ran from that November morning to June 1, 1873, about six months from first shots to Kintpuash's capture.

## Trials and Exile, October 1873

Kintpuash, Schonchin John, Black Jim, and Boston Charley were hanged at Fort Klamath on October 3, 1873. By the NPS count, the war had by then cost 53 U.S. soldiers, 17 civilians, and 2 Warm Springs Scouts their lives. Modoc losses totaled 5 women and children and 15 warriors, 5 of the warriors killed in battle.

The Army sent 163 Modoc to the Quapaw Agency in Indian Territory, in present-day Oklahoma. Over the years that followed, the exiled population fell by about a third, largely to tuberculosis.

## Volcano Underfoot: 500,000 Years of Eruptions

The battlefield sits inside a landscape built by roughly 500,000 years of eruptions from the Medicine Lake volcano. The NPS describes it as a 900-square-mile highland tied to the Cascades. The same activity built cinder cones and spatter cones across the monument, alongside its basalt and andesite lava flows. Those flows hold more than 750 documented lava tube caves.

## Petroglyph Point: 5,000 Carvings

At Petroglyph Point, a cliff of volcanic tuff carries more than 5,000 individual carvings. The NPS estimates some of the images at over 6,000 years old. The site marks the center of a Modoc creation story.

## From Battlefield to Monument, 1925-1972

President Calvin Coolidge proclaimed Lava Beds a national monument on November 21, 1925. It covers 46,692 acres. Ninety-four percent of that land lies in Siskiyou County, California. The National Park Service took over management of the monument in 1933. In 1972, 28,460 of those acres, 61 percent of the monument, were designated wilderness.

The trenches and cave mouths a visitor passes today are the same ones roughly 50 to 60 Modoc fighters held for months.
