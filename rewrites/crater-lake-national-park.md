# Crater Lake National Park

The rim falls away to the water below. From the south rim you look down 1,943 feet to the water. Wizard Island rises from the water, a cinder cone built inside the caldera after the mountain fell. This is the deepest lake in the United States, in NPS's words, and the deepest volcanic lake in the world. It fills a hole where a mountain used to stand.

## Mount Mazama stood 12,000 feet, then fell ~7,700 years ago

Mount Mazama rose about 12,000 feet before the end. Then it erupted. About 7,700 years ago the volcano expelled roughly 50 cubic kilometers of magma — around 100 times the volume of new magma that Mount St. Helens erupted in 1980, according to the USGS, the largest Cascade eruption in the past million years. It came in two stages. First a single vent drove a Plinian column some 50 kilometers into the sky, laying down pumice and ash. Then ring vents opened around the summit, and pyroclastic flows ran as far as 70 kilometers down the valleys. The summit collapsed into the emptied chamber. Rain and snow filled it. What remained is a caldera roughly 8 by 10 kilometers across and about 1.2 kilometers deep. Hillman Peak, the highest point on the rim today, stands about 8,151 feet; Mount Scott, higher still at 8,929 feet, sits off the rim inside the park.

The ash traveled far. It blanketed much of the Pacific Northwest, and the Mazama tephra is a marker bed geologists trace across western Canada, into Alberta and Saskatchewan.

## Giiwas: the Klamath name, and a battle NPS still retells

The Klamath call the lake Giiwas, a sacred place. The collapsed mountain they name Tum-sum-ne — glossed "the big mountain with top cut off." The Klamath Tribes carry an oral tradition of the eruption, and NPS retells it. Llao, Chief of the Below World, dwelt inside Mazama. Skell, Chief of the Above World, stood at Mount Shasta. The two fought over a Makalak chief's daughter, named Loha in the ethnographic record, hurling what NPS calls "red hot rocks as large as hills." The mountain came down on Llao.

NPS states plainly that Klamath and Umpqua oral traditions indicate people witnessed the eruption 7,700 years ago. Luther Cressman found the deeper proof. He excavated twined sagebrush-bark sandals at Fort Rock Cave in southeast Oregon, stratified beneath a layer of Mazama ash and radiocarbon-dated to roughly 9,100 to 10,400 years. The sandals prove people lived in the region long before the mountain fell. They do not, by themselves, prove anyone watched it.

## A holy site, and a dispossession from 1864 to 1986

NPS reports a belief that the site was so holy that looking upon it could bring death, and that some Native people still choose not to view the lake. It is a belief held by some, not a rule for all. W.G. Steel went further. He wrote that "no Indian ever looked upon Crater Lake" — a romanticized misreading, since earlier records document Klamath people visiting the rim to mourn and to pray.

The documents are plain. The 1864 treaty ceded more than 23 million acres of Klamath, Modoc, and Yahooskin ancestral land, reserving hunting, fishing, and gathering rights. The park was created on May 22, 1902, without tribal consent — the fifth national park. Congress terminated the Tribes in 1954. Congress restored their recognition in 1986.

The water is still there, 1,943 feet deep, below the rim.
