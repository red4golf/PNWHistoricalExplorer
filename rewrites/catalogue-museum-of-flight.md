## Facts corrected

1. **the original Boeing Red Barn, carefully relocated from its Georgetown site in 1975**
   - Problem: Wrong neighborhood. The Red Barn's original site was on the WEST bank of the Duwamish, in McLaughlin's Waterfront Addition, just north of South Park and ACROSS the river from Georgetown — not in Georgetown.
   - Corrected to: Shipwright Edward W. Heath bought Lots 3, 4, 5 and 6 of Block 33, McLaughlin's Waterfront Addition, in August 1909 — bottomland 'platted... on the west bank of the river just north of the community of South Park and across from Georgetown.' The site sat 'about five miles south of the river's mouth at Elliott Bay,' at the base of a prominent oxbow. Boeing later called it 'the Oxbow'; it became Plant 1 after 1936.
   - Source: Tukwila Landmark Registration Form, Dec. 13, 2017, p. 5 *(primary-official)*
2. **(implied/prior-draft trap) the Red Barn floated down the Duwamish**
   - Problem: DIRECTION TRAP. The Duwamish empties north into Elliott Bay; the barn moved from a site five miles above the mouth to Boeing Field, which is further south. It went UPSTREAM/SOUTH.
   - Corrected to: Record only the nomination's wording: on December 16, 1975 the building was 'braced, jacked up, set on wheels, and rolled to the river's edge. From there it was loaded onto a Foss Company barge and floated two miles south, passing under the First Avenue South bascule bridge, all the way to Boeing Field.' The nomination's alternate phrasing is 'delivered two miles upstream to Boeing Field.' The distance 'two miles' IS in this document — but it is stated as two miles south/upstream, not downriver, and not any other figure.
   - Source: Tukwila Landmark Registration Form, Dec. 13, 2017, pp. 7 and 22 *(primary-official)*
3. **restored to its 1910 appearance**
   - Problem: Wrong target year. The 1980-83 rehabilitation restored the building to roughly its 1917 configuration, and the recreated second-floor offices were based on 1916-1918 drawings and photographs.
   - Corrected to: 'Although the building evolved in form and function over the years, its appearance today is reflective of about 1917.' The painted 'Boeing Airplane Co.' sign on the west facade 'first appeared on the building in 1917.'
   - Source: Tukwila Landmark Registration Form, Dec. 13, 2017, pp. 4, 8 *(primary-official)*
4. **The museum began modestly in a rented hangar at Boeing Field, displaying a small collectio**
   - Problem: Not supported by any graded source, and contradicted by the documented record. The first museum was at Seattle Center, not Boeing Field, and not in a hangar.
   - Corrected to: 'In May 1968, the PNAHF opened a seasonal Museum of Flight in Building 50 at the Seattle Center. The building had only recently housed a covered area for the Seattle World's Fair amusement park. It offered an expansive, 10,000 square-foot space for exhibits, displays, and five full-sized aircraft. Until 1973, the museum operated only in the summer.'
   - Source: Tukwila Landmark Registration Form, Dec. 13, 2017, p. 19 *(primary-official)*
5. **founded in 1965 by a group of aviation enthusiasts who recognized the need to preserve the**
   - Problem: Vague and anonymous; the founder is documented by name. Note also that the museum's own history page says 'In 1964, a small group of aviation enthusiasts...' while its 2015 press boilerplate says it was 'Celebrating its 50th anniversary in 2015.' The nomination, citing the Oct. 22, 1965 Post-Intelligencer, is the precise account.
   - Corrected to: 'Brackin took action toward that end in 1965, when he founded a dynamic organization called the Pacific Northwest Aviation Historical Foundation (PNAHF). The group was incorporated in September, 1965, in Renton, Washington, with 12 well-connected commercial aviation leaders, bankers, and public relations men as trustees.' Harl V. Brackin, Jr. joined Boeing in 1942 as a wind-tunnel engineer and became the company's first corporate historian.
   - Source: Tukwila Landmark Registration Form, Dec. 13, 2017, pp. 18-19 *(primary-official)*
6. **The choice of Boeing Field as the museum's location was strategic**
   - Problem: Reads as design; the record shows the site was the last of many contingent options after years of failed alternatives.
   - Corrected to: Sites considered included Renton Airport, Lake Sammamish State Park, Cedar Hills, Marymoor Park in Redmond, Sand Point, and a five-acre Boeing land donation in South Park. King County came to the table as a partner only at the end of 1974; the County had to buy land from nine private owners, and 'Final acquisition of all the necessary project parcels dragged on until March 1979.'
   - Source: Tukwila Landmark Registration Form, Dec. 13, 2017, pp. 19-22 *(primary-official)*
7. **The 3.5-acre gallery, with its soaring glass walls and innovative suspension system**
   - Problem: '3.5-acre' is unsupported; the museum states volume and stories, not acreage. (3 acres is the Aviation Pavilion figure — likely a conflation.)
   - Corrected to: '3 million-cubic-foot, six-story, glass-and-steel exhibit hall,' 'currently containing 39 full-size historic aircraft.'
   - Source: The Museum of Flight, T.A. Wilson Great Gallery page *(primary-official)*
8. **houses the world's most comprehensive collection of aircraft in a single space**
   - Problem: Unsupported superlative; nobody makes this claim, including the museum.
   - Corrected to: Drop, or use the museum's own attributed and softer wording: 'The Museum of Flight's collection is considered one of the best air and space museum collections in the world.'
   - Source: The Museum of Flight, Collections & Research page *(primary-official)*
9. **## World War II Gallery — The museum's World War II Gallery presents one of the world's mo**
   - Problem: No such gallery exists. The museum's gallery list is: Great Gallery, Red Barn, Personal Courage Wing, Space Gallery, Aviation Pavilion, Vietnam Veterans Memorial Park. The whole section header is fabricated, and the superlative is unsupported.
   - Corrected to: WWI and WWII fighters are in the J. Elroy McCaw Personal Courage Wing (88,000 sq ft, opened June 6, 2004, 28 aircraft, two floors). The heavy bombers — B-17F Flying Fortress, B-29 Superfortress and B-47 Stratojet — are in the Aviation Pavilion.
   - Source: The Museum of Flight, Museum Galleries index, Personal Courage Wing page, Aviation Pavilion page *(primary-official)*
10. **The Personal Courage Wing features World War I aircraft in a immersive environment**
   - Problem: Understates it — the wing covers both wars, on two floors.
   - Corrected to: 'A two-story gallery that highlights the stories of courage, dedication, heroism of those involved in fighter aviation in World War I and World War II.'
   - Source: The Museum of Flight, Museum Galleries index and Personal Courage Wing page *(primary-official)*
11. **enemy aircraft including a Japanese Zero fighter and German Me 163 rocket-powered intercep**
   - Problem: Neither aircraft appears in the museum's own Air & Spacecraft index. The Me 163 appears only in the museum's archives/photo collections, not as a held airframe.
   - Corrected to: The museum's Axis holdings include a Nakajima Ki-43-IIIa Hayabusa 'Oscar' Reproduction (Japanese Army fighter — a reproduction, not an original), a Messerschmitt Bf 109 E 'Emil,' and a Fieseler Fi 103 'V1.'
   - Source: The Museum of Flight, Air & Spacecraft index (museumofflight.org/exhibits-and-events/aircraft/) *(primary-official)*
12. **featuring authentic spacecraft... The collection includes a Apollo command module**
   - Problem: Implies a flown spacecraft. It is a ground-test article that never went to space.
   - Corrected to: North American Aviation Apollo Command Module 007A — 'the first production-line capsule delivered to NASA for testing and training.' It never flew in space. It was used for impact and acoustic testing at Downey, water impact and flotation trials in the Gulf of Mexico, hatch qualification, and a 48-hour open-water test crewed by astronauts Lovell, Roosa and Duke in April 1968. 'Stored in Houston until 1988, when it was restored for the Museum by the Kansas Cosmosphere and Space Center.' The museum also holds an Apollo 17 Lunar Module Ascent Stage Mock-up.
   - Source: The Museum of Flight, Apollo Command Module 007A page and Air & Spacecraft index *(primary-official)*
13. **space shuttle trainer**
   - Problem: Too vague, and risks implying a flown orbiter.
   - Corrected to: NASA's Space Shuttle Full Fuselage Trainer — 'a full-scale mockup of the space shuttle orbiter, minus the wings,' built at Johnson Space Center in the 1970s and 'the oldest mockup in the Space Vehicle Mockup Facility.' Its 28-foot-long crew cabin section arrived aboard NASA's Super Guppy cargo plane on June 30, 2012; it 'has been on exhibit at the Museum since 2013.'
   - Source: The Museum of Flight, NASA Full Fuselage Trainer page; MoF press releases June 2012 and Feb. 5, 2025; NASA news release 'NASA to Hand Over Shuttle Trainer Keys to Seattle Museum' *(primary-official)*
14. **attracting over 500,000 visitors annually**
   - Problem: No current figure is published by any graded source. The museum's own boilerplate numbers are a decade or more old and inconsistent with each other, and predate the pandemic.
   - Corrected to: Museum boilerplate, 2012: 'attracting more than 400,000 visitors annually.' Museum boilerplate, 2015: 'serving more than 560,000 visitors annually.' If used at all, attribute AND date it ('the museum reported more than 560,000 visitors a year in 2015'). Otherwise drop.
   - Source: MoF press releases, ShuttleFest 2012 boilerplate and Jan. 27 / Apr. 6, 2015 boilerplate *(primary-official)*
15. **The museum's research library contains one of the world's largest collections of aviation **
   - Problem: Overstated. The museum's own claim is regional, not global.
   - Corrected to: 'The Museum's aviation and space library and archives are the largest on the West Coast.' Holdings: approximately 40,000 books, 80,000 journal issues, 18,000 aircraft and accessory manuals, 7,000 technical reports, and 'nearly 5,000 cubic feet of archival materials, including over a million images,' plus 'over 25,000 small objects.'
   - Source: MoF press boilerplate, Jan. 27, 2015; The Museum of Flight, Collections & Research page *(primary-official)*
16. **the world's largest independent, non-profit air and space museum (title description and op**
   - Problem: This is the museum's own marketing self-description, not an independently graded finding. Its own 2015 press boilerplate used the weaker 'one of the largest air and space museums in the world.'
   - Corrected to: Attribute in the text: the museum describes itself as 'the largest independent, non-profit air and space museum in the world' (a phrasing also carried on the Smithsonian Affiliations locator entry, which reproduces museum-supplied copy). Do not assert it in the site's own voice.
   - Source: The Museum of Flight, About page; Smithsonian Affiliations locator, my.si.edu; cf. MoF press boilerplate Jan. 27, 2015 *(primary-official)*
17. **housing more than 130 aircraft and spacecraft**
   - Problem: The museum publishes conflicting figures across its own pages; the writer should pick one and cite the page.
   - Corrected to: 'Over 130 aircraft and spacecraft' (About page, current). '150 aircraft' (Collections & Research page, current). 'More than 160 historically significant airplanes and spacecraft' (2015 press boilerplate). Safest: 'more than 130' with the About page cited, or 'around 150' with Collections & Research cited.
   - Source: The Museum of Flight, About page and Collections & Research page *(primary-official)*
18. **replica Wright Flyers**
   - Problem: Fine in substance but should be labelled precisely; these are the museum's own designations.
   - Corrected to: 'Wright 1903 Flyer Reproduction' and 'Wright 1902 Glider Reproduction.' The museum also holds an 'Otto Lilienthal 1893 Glider Reproduction' and an 'Octave Chanute 1896 Biplane Glider Reproduction.'
   - Source: The Museum of Flight, Air & Spacecraft index *(primary-official)*
19. **a P-51D Mustang, the fighter that helped win air superiority over Europe**
   - Problem: The museum itself hedges this airframe's identity, and it is in the Personal Courage Wing, not the (nonexistent) WWII Gallery.
   - Corrected to: 'The aircraft is likely serial number 44-72423, which was built by North American Aviation in 1944.' It 'displays the colors and markings of Lieutenant Colonel Glenn T. Eagleston' of the 353rd Fighter Squadron, and postwar served with the Swedish Air Force and the Israeli Air Force (IDF/AF 146). Displayed in the Personal Courage Wing.

## New material found in research

- THE MOVE, EXACTLY. The Port of Seattle 'lifted and rolled the Red Barn 600 feet to a corner of the site' on January 30, 1975, where it sat on shorings for ten months while volunteers stripped off later additions. Then, on December 16, 1975, 'the old factory with its original shed wing was braced, jacked up, set on wheels, and rolled to the river's edge. From there it was loaded onto a Foss Company barge and floated two miles south, passing under the First Avenue South bascule bridge, all the way to Boeing Field.' Final cost of the move: $32,000. It then sat at a temporary Boeing Field site for nearly five years, and was not trucked onto its permanent foundation until November 24, 1980. [Tukwila Landmark Registration Form, pp. 7, 21-23]
- THE BUILDING BEFORE BOEING. In August 1909 the shipwright Edward W. Heath — born 1863 into a New Bedford shipbuilding family, in the trade since 23, advertising in Michigan as a 'General Ship Builder' for 'barges, yachts, and upper cabin steamers' — bought four lots on the Duwamish about five miles above the river's mouth at Elliott Bay, at the base of an oxbow. By October he had built a 60-by-140-foot boat shed with a 26-foot wharf and a 10-foot launch way, partly on pilings out over the water, its river-side wall 'composed of massive double doors leading to an expansive interior open from the dirt floor all the way to the roof structure.' Heath went broke building a motor yacht for William E. Boeing, who bought the yard from him on March 23, 1910. Boeing's people called it 'Heath's,' then 'the Oxbow.' [Tukwila Landmark Registration Form, pp. 5, 13, 15]
- THE SECOND FLOOR THAT CAME AND WENT. In late 1916 Boeing's Pacific Aero Products Company installed a second floor in the boat shed — 5,600 square feet, one-third partitioned into offices, two-thirds left open for manufacturing, with drawings detailing office counters, tongue-and-groove partitions, lighting and employee lockers. That autumn the workforce 'doubled in size from six to 12,' and Boeing hired Wong Tsoo, 'a young Chinese aeronautical engineer fresh out of M.I.T.' In 1937 the second floor was TORN OUT again to make room for a full-scale mock-up of the Boeing 307 Stratoliner; it was re-installed by 1942. [Tukwila Landmark Registration Form, pp. 5-6, 16]
- HARL BRACKIN. In 1962 a Boeing executive told 39-year-old Harl V. Brackin, Jr. — who had joined Boeing in 1942 as a wind-tunnel engineer and become the company's first corporate historian — that the Red Barn was to be demolished. In his own words it was 'all that this dreamer-author' needed. He founded the Pacific Northwest Aviation Historical Foundation in 1965, incorporated that September in Renton with 12 trustees. He died in October 1977 at 54, six years before the museum opened. On September 1, 1983, his widow Emma Brackin and William Boeing, Jr. cut the ribbon together, with Governor John Spellman and King County Executive Randy Revelle looking on. [Tukwila Landmark Registration Form, pp. 18-19, 22-23]
- THE ARGUMENT ABOUT AUTHENTICITY. State preservation officials fought the restoration. In 1977 David Hansen at the Washington Office of Archaeology and Historic Preservation, and initially Acting State Historic Preservation Officer Jeanne Welch, 'believed that too much original fabric was being replaced, and that the old building was essentially being encased in a replica of itself to meet climate control goals.' The museum's backers flew to Washington, D.C. that autumn to meet the National Park Service in a session arranged through Senator Warren Magnuson's office. The project went ahead as designed. The nomination itself concedes the work was 'questioned by some at the time as over-restoration.' [Tukwila Landmark Registration Form, pp. 22, 24]
- THE FOUNDING AIRPLANE. The Boeing 80A-1 in the Great Gallery is the only survivor of the 16 Model 80s built. It was 'recovered from an Anchorage dump in 1960' and flown to Seattle aboard a U.S. Air Force C-124. Per the museum: 'The Model 80A-1 restoration project served as a catalyst for establishment of the Pacific Northwest Aviation Historical Foundation, which later became The Museum of Flight.' Within its first year the Foundation had acquired both a 1929 Boeing Model 80A and a 1933 Model 247D — both still on the campus. [MoF Boeing 80A-1 page; Tukwila Landmark Registration Form, p. 19]
- ALPHA GOLF. Concorde G-BOAG, the eighth British-built production Concorde, first flew in April 1978 and logged more than 5,600 takeoffs and over 16,200 flight hours. It flew the last British Airways commercial Concorde service, New York to London, on October 24, 2003. Twelve days later, on its retirement delivery flight to the Museum on November 5, 2003, it set a New York-to-Seattle speed record of 3 hours, 55 minutes and 2 seconds. [MoF Concorde page]
- SAM 970. The Boeing VC-137B parked in the Aviation Pavilion, tail number 58-6970, was the first presidential jet: 'Eisenhower became the first U.S. president to fly by jet on SAM 970 on August 26, 1959.' It carried Eisenhower, Kennedy, Johnson and Nixon, flew Henry Kissinger to the North Vietnamese peace talks in 1970-71, to the secret China talks in 1971, and on Middle East shuttle diplomacy in 1974, and stayed in the presidential fleet until June 1996. It is on loan from the National Museum of the U.S. Air Force. [MoF Boeing VC-137B page]
- AIRFRAMES WITH DATES ON THEM. Boeing 747 RA001 (N7470), the first 747 ever built, first flew February 9, 1969 and was donated to the Museum on its final flight, April 6, 1995, after 5,300 flight hours. The Lockheed M-21 (60-6940) is the sole survivor of two built — the other was lost in a D-21 drone launch accident in 1966 — acquired in 1991, on loan from the National Museum of the U.S. Air Force. The B-17F 'Boeing Bee,' serial 42-29782, was built at Boeing's Plant 2 in Seattle on February 13, 1943, later worked as a crop sprayer and fire bomber, appeared in 'Tora! Tora! Tora!' (1970) and 'Memphis Belle' (1989), and flew from Renton to Boeing Field in 1998 for permanent display. In the Great Gallery a nine-ton Douglas DC-3 hangs from the space-frame ceiling in flight attitude, one of 39 full-size aircraft under a 3-million-cubic-foot, six-story glass-and-steel roof. [MoF aircraft and gallery pages]
- WHAT THE RED BARN SAYS NOW. Its first exhibits, in place from 1983 to 1987, were designed by Seattle industrial designer Gideon Kramer and built by Promotion Products, Inc. of Portland; they included a meticulous scale model of Plant 1 based on a 1918 map drawn by Boeing draftswoman Helen Holcombe, salvaged from the site. Updated in 2005 and again in 2016 for Boeing's centennial, the current exhibits cover the Boeing Model C, the straightening of the Duwamish channel, women in aviation, and Wong Tsoo. In 1969, nominating the building to the National Register, Margaret Corley found it in use as facilities maintenance shops and a warehouse, with 'minor modifications in window arrangements,' but insisted 'the basic structure is original and in sound condition.' It was listed August 26, 1971 — nominated in the same King County batch as Pioneer Square, the Pike Place Market, the schooner Wawona and Pioneer Hall. [Tukwila Landmark Registration Form, pp. 6, 19-20, 23-24; NPS NPGallery 71000872]

## Final editorial pass — read like a guide, not a paper (2026-07-26)

Charles's note: the entry should help a visitor learn about and enjoy the site,
not read like a research paper. The verified draft failed that test — it carried
document-citation scaffolding in the prose ("The Lentz and Martin form says…",
"That same 2017 form words the trip two ways…"), spec-sheet passages, and no
address to the visitor at all.

**What changed.** Direct quotation of municipal paperwork was removed and the
facts restated plainly; provenance now lives in the Sources block, where it
belongs. Roughly 200 words of building-committee history were traded for aircraft
the pack had already sourced and the draft had ignored: the Boeing 80A-1 pulled
from an Anchorage dump, SAM 970 and Eisenhower's first jet flight, Concorde
G-BOAG's last commercial service, and RA001 donated on its own final flight. The
entry now opens by telling the reader where to start.

**The rewrite was itself verified**, and drifted — proof that warmth is where
unsourced detail enters. Eight substantive corrections were required, all fixed
from the pack: Wong Tsoo was placed among the twelve new hires (the pack never
says so); "believed" was hardened to "argued" and "essentially" dropped from the
preservation officials' objection; the museum's own claim about the 80A-1
restoration was promoted into the site's voice; the Register batch was a
*nomination* batch, not a listing batch; "commercial" was dropped from the
