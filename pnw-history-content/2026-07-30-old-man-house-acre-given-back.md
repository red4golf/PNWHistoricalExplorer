In 1870 federal officials ordered the destruction of Old Man House, the cedar longhouse that stood on the beach at Suquamish, directly across Agate Passage from the north end of Bainbridge Island. It was the winter village of the Suquamish and the home of Chief Seattle and Chief Kitsap. Accounts of its size differ. HistoryLink puts it at roughly 900 feet long and 60 feet wide, divided into 40 apartments. The Suquamish Museum simply calls it the largest longhouse in the region.

The official reason was public health. The effect was assimilation. Communal living was what federal policy of that era set out to break, and taking down the house did it. The Suquamish kept living on the ground where it had stood until 1904, when they were pushed onto individual family allotments. That same year, poor and squeezed, the tribe sold the beach to the U.S. Army. Developers owned it after that. In 1950 it became Old Man House State Park, one acre of shell-strewn sand. An interpretive sign installed in 1975 described the Suquamish as people who "once lived in the area."

They had never left.

On August 12, 2004, the Washington State Parks and Recreation Commission voted to give the acre to the Suquamish Tribe. It was the first time the state had ever given land to a tribe. The seven-member commission was unanimous. The transfer came with conditions. The tribe promised to spend $50,000 over five years on maintenance, and the state kept the right to take the park back if the land were privatized or the agreed hours ignored.

Not everyone in town wanted it. One neighborhood group fought the transfer and another worked alongside the tribe. Reverend Tom Thresher of the Suquamish Community Congregational United Church of Christ told the commission, "You have an opportunity to heal old wounds, and I think reach out a hand in friendship today that I think will heal our community." Rob Purser, the tribal member who led the effort, called it "a good step in the healing process for the tribe."

When the decision came down, the commission had to recess. Tribal members sang a song of thanks and drummed. About twenty Suquamish who had arrived by canoe beat their paddles on the floor.

Old Man House Park is still there, at 17818 South Angeline Avenue NE in Suquamish. Still one acre, still open, still a beach. A short walk away stands the House of Awakened Culture, dedicated in February 2009 and built to honor the longhouse that burned. It was the first traditional communal space the Suquamish had in 139 years. Chief Seattle's grave is up the hill in the Suquamish cemetery, where a graveside ceremony each August anchors Chief Seattle Days, held in town since 1911.
