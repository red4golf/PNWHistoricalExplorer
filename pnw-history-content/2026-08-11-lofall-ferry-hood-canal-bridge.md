On August 12, 1961, thousands of people sat in a five-mile traffic backup on Hood Canal waiting to drive across a brand new bridge. It was opening 15 months behind schedule. The floating section ran 6,520 feet on 23 concrete pontoons, the longest bridge of its kind in the world, and the first one anybody had tried to float on salt water with tides that swing 16 feet.

The celebration had a quiet cost about a mile to the south. That was the day the state ferry dock at Lofall went out of business.

The dock was there because a commercial fisherman built it. Bill Lofall and his wife Martha owned the beach property, and in 1949 the Black Ball Line asked to put a ferry landing on it. Bill built the dock and the road down to it, still named Wesley Way. Black Ball supplied the pilings and the mechanical gear. The landing was commissioned on June 10, 1950, and by 1951 the state had taken over Black Ball's Puget Sound routes.

Three buildings served the crossing. There was a ferry waiting room with a single office. There was a bait house where Bill and Martha sold herring to the fishing fleet. And there was the Sea Breeze Inn, a restaurant Bill barged over from Scandia and set on the dock, where Ruth Ryan sold hot food to people waiting for a boat. Her husband Red ran the tavern and gas station at the Lofall four corners.

The boats came and went. The wooden ferry Kitsap, good for about 30 cars, worked the run its entire eleven years. The wooden Vashon made 28 crossings a day until 1954. Then came the Rhododendron, which had been the Gov. Herbert R. O'Conner on Chesapeake Bay until a bridge there put her out of work. Washington bought her, towed her through the Panama Canal, and renamed her for the state flower. The trip across took ten minutes.

Then the bridge opened and the run was retired. The Lofalls got their property back. The waiting room and the Sea Breeze Inn became rental houses. In 1973 neighbors formed the Lofall Community Corporation and bought the place.

Six years later, on the morning of February 13, 1979, the west half of the bridge broke loose in a windstorm and sank. WSDOT came knocking at Lofall again. The state built a passenger pier, then rebuilt the dock to land car ferries, and those boats moved about 3,100 vehicles a day until the bridge reopened in October 1982. They were retired within days. Then in 2009, during the east half replacement, the state came back a third time.

The dock is private now, so admire it from the road. But drive down Wesley Way and you can see where a fisherman's beach did the state's work three separate times. Salsbury Point Park, a mile west of Port Gamble, puts you on the water at the bridge's east end.
