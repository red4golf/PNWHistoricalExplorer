On August 30, 1919, a bill of sale changed hands in a small settlement on the north Kitsap shoreline. The price was one dollar. The item was a wooden dock, and the buyer was the new Port of Eglon.

Eglon sits on the east side of the north Kitsap Peninsula, five miles north of Kingston and three miles south of Point No Point. Families settled there early in the twentieth century and called the place Silver Creek, after homesteaders Charles and Jessie Silver, until they applied for a post office in 1906 and found the name was taken. Eglon, out of the Old Testament, won because it was the shortest name on the list.

Before there was a dock, there was rowing. Mosquito Fleet steamers passed the beach several times a day on their runs between Seattle and the towns around the sound. In decent weather someone rowed out and handed across a passenger or a sack of mail. In bad weather you stayed aboard and tried again tomorrow. Livestock were swum ashore or herded up the beach from Kingston.

Residents built their first dock in 1913 through the Eglon Dock Company. By 1918 upkeep had fallen to too few volunteers and the thing was unsafe, so they tore it down and started over. A private Community Dock Company put up a replacement that fall, and the Puget Sound Navigation Company announced that from November 10, 1918, "the Steamer PUGET will land at the new dock in Eglon in both directions."

On July 12, 1919, eleven registered voters turned out to decide whether to create a port district. Ten said yes. Gust Sevon, Nikolai Naykki, and Henry Johnson became the commissioners, the county canvassed the returns on July 21, and the dock came over for that dollar at month's end. Naykki took the paid job of wharfinger, and out of his salary he had to furnish the coal oil for the dock's night light.

The dock did not last. The first car ferry ran from Kingston in 1923, the county highway reached Eglon in 1924, and scheduled boat service ended by 1933. The dock came down by the early 1940s. The port outlived it. By 1940 it had stopped collecting taxes, and its last expenditure for years was fifty dollars on lumber for a bathhouse in 1945.

What it would not do was dissolve. When Olympia floated folding Washington's small ports into one statewide authority in 1960, Eglon commissioner George Coles told a legislative hearing where his community stood. "We don't want any industry," he said. "Leave us alone. We don't want to be infringed upon. We want to keep our little bit of beach and use it for the children to swim." Kitsap County had twenty one port districts then. By 2011 it was down to twelve, still more than any other county in the state.

The consolidation plan died. Eglon's port did not. Go north from Kingston today and you can park above the beach, launch a boat for free, and sit at a picnic table beside a fire pit on the hundred feet of shoreline the port kept after the steamers quit calling. Neighbors still gather there Wednesday nights all summer. It is not much of a port. That was always the point.
