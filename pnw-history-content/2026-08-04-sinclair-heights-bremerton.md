On August 1, 1944, the Bremerton Housing Authority counted 342 Black families in its care. Two hundred seventy-nine of them lived in the same place.

That place was Sinclair Park, 280 prefabricated units on 80 acres atop a hill west of the city, named for the inlet below. Locals called it Sinclair Heights. By that August it was full. Of the 280 homes, 279 held Black families. The last one held a Filipino family.

The arithmetic behind that number is stark. Bremerton's 1940 census counted seven Black residents. By 1945 the city had more than 4,500, pulled west by shipyard work after Pearl Harbor. Federal policy did not call for segregation, and the nine new developments around Bremerton and Port Orchard were planned as integrated. Then white residents objected to Black neighbors, and the housing authority quietly made Sinclair Park the place Black families were assigned.

It was a hard place to live. Construction started in the summer of 1942, and the first residents, Eugene Aires and Elwood Greer, moved in during March 1943. There was no landscaping and there were no streetlights, and people remembered the dark for decades afterward. The school bus wouldn't come up the hill because the neighborhood sat a mile outside the district. There was exactly one telephone, in the community center.

That community center is where the story turns. Built in 1943 for about $57,000, it was among the first buildings designed by the young Seattle firm of Naramore, Bain, Brady and Johanson, later NBBJ. Volunteers ran a night library in it twice a week. Voting booths went up in it at election time. Lilian Walker started Bremerton's NAACP branch inside it. A drama club staged "Jiving Macbeth" on its small stage.

On that stage sat an upright piano. In 1944 an eleven-year-old named Quincy Jones broke in with friends to steal a pie, wandered into the recreation room, and found it. "I went up there, paused, stared, and then tinkled on it for a moment," he wrote in 2001. "That's where I began to find peace." He kept sneaking back until a sympathetic supervisor started leaving the door unlocked.

The neighborhood was always meant to be temporary. The houses were built from panels so they'd come apart fast. Eighty units went up for sale on July 1, 1946, and by 1948 the rest were gone, some of them floated to waterfront lots around the Kitsap Peninsula and used as summer cabins. What remained was streets, sidewalks, and the community center. That building served as a National Guard armory until it was torn down in late 2002.

The old hilltop site is a stretch of car dealerships now. The rest is downtown. Quincy Square opened on Fourth Street on July 12, 2025, three blocks up from the ferry, with interpretive signs about Sinclair Heights and a bronze of Jones at Fourth and Pacific. In the Kitsap History Museum lobby stands a scuffed Melville Clark upright, said to be the piano from the hill. Nobody has roped it off. You can put your hands on the keys.
