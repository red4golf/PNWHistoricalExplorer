In July 1853, a sea captain from Maine took a small sailboat up Hood Canal, hunting for a place to build a sawmill. William C. Talbot had just spent 171 days bringing the 50 ton schooner Julius Pringle around Cape Horn from East Machias, Maine. He liked Port Ludlow, but another mill man had already claimed it. A few miles on he found a sandspit at the mouth of a two mile bay, sheltered from the prevailing winds and ringed with Douglas fir. That choice, made 173 years ago this month, started what became the longest running sawmill in American history.

The spit wasn't empty. S'Klallam families lived there and on the bluff above, and they called the place Teekalet, meaning brightness of the noonday sun. Talbot borrowed the name for his mill. His partner Josiah Keller later persuaded the S'Klallam to move across the bay to Point Julia in exchange for free lumber, firewood, and Christmas gifts. Many kept working at the mill for generations, commuting to the job by canoe.

Talbot landed ten men, most of them from his hometown, and they put up a bunkhouse, a cookhouse, and a store. The machinery came separately aboard the clipper schooner L.P. Foster with Keller, who took charge of the operation. By September 1853, within a week of landing the engine, boilers, and a single blade muley saw, the crew had steam up and cut their first log. Early output was about 2,000 board feet a day, roughly a tenth of what a five room house of the era required.

Here's a detail worth remembering. Talbot's first Puget Sound lumber shipment didn't come from his own mill at all. He bought pilings from Henry Yesler in Seattle that September while his own saw was still being assembled.

The Puget Mill Company town that grew on the spit was pure transplanted New England. Workers earned about $30 a month and were paid in fifty cent pieces, so reliably that "four bit" coins became a badge of Port Gamble employment around the Sound. In 1859 the company built the first school in Kitsap County. The town took the name Port Gamble in 1868, honoring a Navy lieutenant wounded in the War of 1812.

The mill cut its last log on November 30, 1995, after 142 continuous years, the oldest continuously operating sawmill in the United States.

You can still walk it all today. The townsite, on the National Register since 1966, keeps its general store, its 1879 church, and the Walker-Ames House on the bluff. Across the bay at Point Julia, the Port Gamble S'Klallam Tribe lives on the water its ancestors never really left.
