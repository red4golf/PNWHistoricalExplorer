## Context, evidence, and full story

Before it was a city park, this stretch of Eastgate was one of the busiest little airfields on the Eastside — a private strip built by one family, flown out of for nearly forty years, and then folded, piece by piece, into a Bellevue business park, a Boeing landholding, and eventually parkland again.

### A Bon Marché son builds an airfield

Arthur Alphonsus Nordhoff (1895-1984) came from Seattle's Bon Marché family and joined the U.S. Army Air Service in 1917, serving with the 168th Aero Squadron in World War I, according to the Museum of Flight Archives. Decades later, in 1941, Nordhoff bought a 160-acre parcel in Eastgate — east of what's now 156th Avenue SE and north of the Sunset Highway (later I-90). Wartime restrictions delayed the airfield's opening until 1945, a timeline confirmed by both the Seattle Times and the Museum of Flight archives.

It wasn't glamorous country at the outset. Per Eastside Heritage Center's research, the airfield's original neighbors included a gun club, a turkey ranch, a mink farm, and a garbage dump. Nordhoff served as president of Puget Sound Air Service, Inc., the company that ran the field, and kept it a family operation: his daughter, Nancy (Nordhoff) Dunnam (1923-2017), went to Texas in 1942 and flew with the Women Airforce Service Pilots (WASP) during the war. Afterward, her husband, James "Jim" Dunnam, became the airfield's vice president, and Arthur's son took over as secretary in the mid-1950s after returning from service in the Korean War.

### How busy did it get?

The best-documented measure of the airfield's activity comes from two independent sources agreeing with each other: the Museum of Flight Archives' finding aid for the "Nordhoff and Dunnam Families World War I and Bellevue Airport Collection" states the field "reached its peak in the 1970s when there were on average 51,000 landings and takeoffs a year," and Paul Freeman's Airfields-Freeman.com research, drawing in part on Seattle Times coverage, independently arrives at the same figure — "51,000 takeoffs & landings annually." That average is the field's best-supported activity statistic.

There's also a striking single-year snapshot: Eastside Heritage Center's blog on Eastside airfields, citing a 1969 Bellevue American article, reported that "by 1969, 125 planes occupied the field with '80,000 take-offs and landings in a year.'" That 80,000-operations figure is specific to 1969 rather than a sustained peak — it's a busy single-year count that happens to run well above the multi-year average, not a description of ongoing traffic through the early 1970s. A 1959 FAA inspection cited by Eastside Heritage Center found 82 based aircraft, and an aerial photo from 1980 reportedly showed more than 75 aircraft still parked on the field — down from the 125 counted in 1969, but still a substantial fleet for a field just a few years from closing.

Physically, the field was modest: a single asphalt runway, approximately 2,300 feet long (reported lengths across sources range from about 2,250 to 2,500 feet), with no control tower and no ground navigation aids ever installed, according to Eastside Heritage Center. Runway lights did operate, staying lit until 11 p.m. Over the years the field passed through several operators, including Nordhoff's own Puget Sound Air Service, later Bellevue Aviation, and Eagle Aircraft Service.

### Seattle Flight and the Safeway crash

One of the field's tenants, Seattle Flight, grew into a major regional operation. Per Airfields-Freeman.com, Seattle Flight "eventually grew to become the largest flight school in the Pacific Northwest, with a fleet of over 40 Piper aircraft," and also ran facilities at Boeing Field, Everett-Paine Field, and Auburn Airport.

The airfield's most dramatic recorded incident came on July 31, 1976, according to Feliks Banel's reporting for MyNorthwest: a pilot flying a Cessna, returning from Bellingham, crashed into the front of the Eastgate Safeway store. A few people inside the store were cut by broken glass, and the pilot escaped with an injured elbow. There were no fatalities.

### Closure and the land's second life

By late 1978, the Nordhoff family had sold the airfield land to developer Cabot, Cabot & Forbes, which planned it as the "I-90/Bellevue Business Park" (one source places CC&F's operational takeover in 1979, a discrepancy of only months). The airfield's last flight came on May 2, 1983 — a date corroborated independently by both MyNorthwest and Bellevue Airfield Park's own historical timeline, though Eastside Heritage Center's blog gives February 1983, a three-month discrepancy. On May 21, 1983, the airfield's buildings, hangars, and a Quonset hut went up for public auction — "a giant garage sale," as MyNorthwest described it.

Boeing bought parcels of the former airfield, including a former landfill portion, in 1980. The City of Bellevue later acquired the land in two stages: 14.23 acres (the former landfill parcel) from Boeing in 2002, and 10.51 acres from the former Bellevue School District in 2004. Together those parcels became today's Bellevue Airfield Park, 27.5 acres in all, of which about 11 acres are buildable flat land.

The landfill history is worth knowing on its own: from 1951 to 1964, King County operated the "Eastgate Landfill" on the north end of the airfield property. When landfill operations stopped in 1964, the site was capped with soil over roughly 14 acres. In 2002, the Washington Department of Ecology designated part of the site a Model Toxics Control Act cleanup site, and use restrictions from that designation remain in effect today, per research compiled by Save Airfield Park.

### Where to go deeper

The Museum of Flight Archives holds the "Nordhoff and Dunnam Families World War I and Bellevue Airport Collection," spanning the 1910s to the 1980s with a 2014 addition. It includes 23 aerial photographs of the airfield taken between 1941 and 1985, 47 photographs of people and aircraft, three disassembled scrapbooks covering 1962 to 1980, Nordhoff's own World War I military documents and photos, and other airfield ephemera. A broader "Nordhoff and Dunnam Families Collection," covering 1916 to 2012, is also held by the Museum, and at least one digitized aerial photograph from the collection is viewable in the Museum's digital collections online.

A few physical traces of the airfield reportedly persist near the park today: red-and-white utility poles near the NE 150th Street I-90 overpass once carried flashing warning lights, now long dark, and a small patch of runway or taxiway-marked asphalt — about 50 feet square — has been noted by a member of the Nordhoff family, per MyNorthwest's reporting, as possibly still visible on the site. Nearby, the Aerowood Animal Hospital, named in 1976 by Dr. Donald Kalps, keeps the airfield's name alive in the neighborhood.
