# The Boeing Red Barn's Original Site: Georgetown, 1910-1975

At this Georgetown address on the Duwamish River, nothing survives from the building that became Boeing's first aircraft factory. The barn left this spot on December 16, 1975. William Boeing had bought the building, a former shipyard structure, in 1910, intending it for boat construction and repair. By 1916 the company he and Conrad Westervelt built had produced its first airplane, the B&W, a two-seat floatplane that made its maiden flight off Lake Union on June 15, 1916; during World War I the Georgetown building became the company's manufacturing plant, employing a peak wartime workforce of 337 people.

## A Shipyard Becomes a Factory, 1910-1917

William Boeing moved to Seattle in 1908, after several years in the timber business based in Hoquiam. He went looking for airplanes two years later: in January 1910 he attended America's first international air meet, at Dominguez Field near Los Angeles, and tried and failed to get a ride from the French aviator Louis Paulhan. Boeing's own first flight as a passenger is usually dated to July 4, 1915, on Lake Washington, with pilot Terah Maroney and Navy Lieutenant Conrad Westervelt riding along — though HistoryLink, the source for that date, notes that some historians doubt it, since July 4, 1915 fell on a Sunday.

Boeing had bought the former Heath Shipyard building on the Duwamish River back in 1910, intending it for boat construction and repair. Six years later, he and Westervelt had built an airplane instead. Their B&W, nicknamed the Bluebill, flew for the first time on June 15, 1916, off Lake Union. One month later, on July 15, 1916, Boeing incorporated the company as Pacific Aero Products Co. He renamed it the Boeing Airplane Co. on May 9, 1917. A National Park Service biography later called the firm Boeing founded "the largest and most successful commercial aircraft manufacturer in the world." During World War I, that company's manufacturing plant was this Georgetown building on the Duwamish riverbank.

## Oxbow, Plant 1, the Red Barn

Workers had three names for the building over the years. They called it Oxbow first, then Plant 1 after 1936, then the Red Barn, for the color of its main structure.

During World War I the Navy ordered 50 Model C floatplanes from Boeing, to be used as trainers. To design the improved Model C, the company hired a young Chinese engineer named Wong Tsu — one of its first engineering hires. The Navy order pushed the Red Barn workforce to its wartime peak: 337 workers, building floatplanes inside an old shipyard shed on the riverbank. For a few years that was the whole company: one barn, one river, a few hundred people.

## Two Days on the Duwamish, December 16-17, 1975

In 1975 the Port of Seattle leased land to the young Museum of Flight for 99 years, and the museum arranged to move the barn there. Seattle historian Paul Dorpat gives the building's size: built in 1909, about 150 by 65 feet, weighing roughly 325 tons by the time it moved. The move took two days. A Foss barge carried the barn about two miles up the Duwamish River on December 16, 1975. Crews rolled it across East Marginal Way the next day. Howard Lovering, the museum's first executive director, ran the operation. King County Executive John Spellman had pushed for it to happen. The restored barn did not open to the public until 1983, as the Museum of Flight's first permanent building. Invention & Technology Magazine has called it "believed to be the oldest surviving aircraft manufacturing building in America" — that claim belongs to the magazine, not to this record.

## The Georgetown Site Today

Nothing from the Boeing era marks the Georgetown address now. The barn itself still stands, two miles up the Duwamish from where it started, at the Museum of Flight, open to visitors since 1983. Visitors there can walk through the same rooms where 337 workers once built Navy trainers and where a small company grew large enough, in time, to earn the National Park Service's description above. The building where William Boeing incorporated a company on July 15, 1916, and left again by barge on December 16, 1975, can still be walked through — just not from here.
