## Farmland Before the City

Japanese immigrants first settled the White River Valley in 1893. Families began farming in the Bellevue area around 1898. By the 1920s, Japanese American families were cultivating about 472 acres of mixed crops in the area, according to the Eastside Heritage Center. Their farms supplied roughly 75% of the vegetables sold in Seattle and King County by that decade, per HistoryLink.org.

Land ownership was constrained by law. Washington's 1921 Alien Land Law barred further land purchases by Japanese immigrants, who as first-generation immigrants (Issei) could not become naturalized citizens. Despite the law, 32 Issei landowners had acquired property in Bellevue between 1905 and 1938, according to the City of Bellevue's own history of the community. Named landowners included Tom (Takeo) Matsuoka, Asaichi Tsushima, Itaro Ito, and Takayoshi Suguro.

In 1919, the Takeshita family purchased 13 acres east of the railroad tracks and north of Lake Bellevue. They grew strawberries alongside pole beans, peas, tomatoes, cabbages, cucumbers, celery, and lettuce, per the Eastside Heritage Center.

By 1942, about 150 acres in the area were planted specifically in strawberries, according to the City of Bellevue.

## Getting Produce to Market

The Northern Pacific Railroad built the Wilburton Trestle through the area in 1904. Decades later, the Bellevue Vegetable Growers' Association — led by Tom Matsuoka as president and later business manager — purchased refrigerated rail cars and opened a shipping warehouse next to a Northern Pacific rail siding beginning in 1932-33. The warehouse let growers ship produce out of the region rather than sell only locally, according to HistoryLink.org's account of berry farming in Washington and a City of Bellevue history document.

## The Strawberry Festival

The Bellevue Strawberry Festival began in June 1925 and drew about 3,000 visitors in its first year. By 1935, attendance had grown to roughly 15,000 — nearly five times Bellevue's population at the time, according to HistoryLink.org. The festival ran every year from 1925 through 1941.

## Kokaido Clubhouse

In 1930, the Japanese American community built the Kokaido Clubhouse at 101st Avenue NE and NE 11th Street, on two acres purchased through pooled community donations. The clubhouse hosted Buddhist and Christian services, Japanese language classes, judo instruction through the Bellevue Dojo, and a Nisei youth club called the Seinenkai, according to the Eastside Heritage Center and a photograph held in the Densho Digital Repository (item ddr-densho-28-12).

The building was sold to Augustana Lutheran Church in 1950 for $11,000 and demolished in 1964.

## Forced Removal, 1942

Miller Freeman, a prominent Bellevue businessman, founded Washington's Anti-Japanese League and was a leading advocate both for wartime removal of Japanese Americans and for blocking their return afterward, according to the Densho Encyclopedia and other sourced accounts.

President Franklin Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066 on February 19, 1942, authorizing the removal of Japanese Americans from the West Coast. At the time, about 90% of Bellevue's agricultural workforce was Japanese American. Approximately 60 families — about 300 people — were forced from the area in May 1942.

Bellevue-area families were first sent to the Pinedale Assembly Center near Fresno, California, for temporary detention. Most were then sent to the Tule Lake concentration camp; some were later transferred to the Minidoka camp in Idaho after Tule Lake was converted into a high-security segregation center in 1943. The 1942 Strawberry Festival was cancelled, ending a run that had continued unbroken since 1925.

Accounts differ on how many families returned to Bellevue after the war ended. By 1953, the Suguro family farm had been sold to Safeway, and the Takeshita family property had been sold to the Great Northern Railroad — two of the parcels that became part of downtown Bellevue.

## What Remains

The strawberry fields themselves are gone, replaced by downtown Bellevue's streets and buildings. The City of Bellevue maintains two blueberry farms, Larsen Lake Blueberry Farm and Mercer Slough Blueberry Farm, both established in the 1940s and described by the city's parks department as preserving the area's agricultural heritage generally.

In 2025, the City of Bellevue installed "Emerging Radiance," a permanent public art piece by artist Michelle Kumata, at the King County Library System's Bellevue branch. The 10-by-13-foot farmhouse-shaped sculpture carries painted portraits of nine named Japanese American farmers. QR codes on the piece link to their Densho oral histories, and the work is now part of the city's permanent art collection.

The Bellevue Historical Society revived the Strawberry Festival in 1987.
