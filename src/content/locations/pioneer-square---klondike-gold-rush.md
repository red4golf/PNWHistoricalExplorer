# Pioneer Square and the Klondike Gold Rush

Pioneer Square sits at the south end of downtown Seattle. The neighborhood burned in the Great Seattle Fire on June 6, 1889. Builders rebuilt it over the following years in load-bearing masonry, mostly Richardsonian Romanesque; the Pioneer Building went up between 1889 and 1891. That is the ground the gold rush landed on.

## July 17, 1897: the Portland docks at Schwabacher's Wharf

The steamship Portland reached Seattle on July 17, 1897. It carried Klondike miners and their gold, and it tied up at Schwabacher's Wharf. Reporter Beriah Brown Jr. did not wait for it. He took a tug out to meet the ship, interviewed the miners, and telegraphed his account before the Portland docked. The Seattle Post-Intelligencer ran an extra edition. Its headline read "GOLD! GOLD! GOLD! GOLD!" The text reported "more than a ton of gold." That line hardened into the popular phrase, a ton of gold. The figure understated the cargo. Roughly two tons came off the ship.

The gold itself lay far from Seattle. The Klondike fields are in Canada's Yukon, not Alaska and not Washington. Seattle was the outfitting and departure gateway, nothing more.

## Why the outfitting trade boomed: the one-ton rule

Prospectors could not enter the Yukon empty-handed. The Canadian North-West Mounted Police required each prospector entering the Yukon to carry roughly a year's supplies, about one ton of goods. They enforced it at the Canadian border atop the Chilkoot and White passes. No supplies, no entry. Every stampeder had to buy that ton somewhere, and Seattle sold it.

Erastus Brainerd made sure of that. As secretary of the Seattle Chamber of Commerce's Bureau of Information, he ran the "Gateway to the Klondike" campaign of newspaper placements, circulars, ads, a gold exhibit, and government petitions. Seattle captured roughly three-quarters of the outfitting trade. The mayor took a different path. W.D. Wood resigned mid-term, by telegram from a convention in San Francisco, to chase the gold himself.

## First Avenue South: outfitters, a shoe store, a woolen shop

Cooper & Levy outfitted stampeders at 104-106 First Avenue South. Its ads warned, "You are going to a country where grub is more valuable than gold." Schwabacher Bros. & Company, a wholesale and hardware firm in the city since 1869, sold goods too; its dock had received the Portland. MacDougall & Southwick was Seattle's earliest department store, not a dedicated outfitter. The boom lifted its general business anyway.

Two other names started here. Clinton C. Filson founded his Seattle outfitting business in 1897, first under the name Seattle Woolen Manufacturing Company; it later became the C.C. Filson Company. John W. Nordstrom sailed for the Klondike in 1897 as well. He netted about $13,000 after selling a disputed Gold Run claim. In 1901 he co-founded the shoe store Wallin & Nordstrom with Carl Wallin at 4th Avenue and Pike Street.

## 42,837 in 1890, 237,194 by 1910

Seattle grew. The city counted 42,837 residents in 1890 and 80,671 in 1900, nearly double. Then it nearly tripled, to 237,194 by 1910. The rush belongs to the first of those jumps. The larger surge came after.

## The Tanana, and the cost in the north

The bill fell elsewhere. Disease spread among the Tanana and other northern Native peoples through trading contact, and Native communities were displaced. The record of Seattle's boom and the record of that harm are one record.

## What stands at 319 Second Avenue South

The Klondike Gold Rush National Historical Park, Seattle Unit, occupies the Cadillac Hotel at 319 Second Avenue South. It serves as the unit's visitor center and museum. The building stands in the Pioneer Square Preservation District. It is one of the masonry structures raised after the fire of 1889, on the same rebuilt ground the Portland's news reached in 1897.
