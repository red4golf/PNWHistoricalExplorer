# Fort Steele Heritage Town: A Fort Built to Enforce a Boundary

You stand on a bluff above the Kootenay River, at a river crossing that has carried three names in under thirty years. None of them lasted long. Galbraith's Ferry became Fort Steele in 1888, named for the Mounted Police officer whose post here had been called Kootenay Post, and by 1898 the Canadian Pacific Railway picked Cranbrook instead, draining the town until the province bought the site in 1961 and rebuilt it into the 60-plus buildings you walk through now.

## Ktunaxa Territory Before the Ferry

The Ktunaxa Nation's traditional territory, ʔamak̓is Ktunaxa, covers roughly 70,000 square kilometres of what is now southeastern British Columbia, Alberta, Idaho, and Montana, centred on the Kootenay and Columbia Rivers and the Arrow Lakes. Upper Ktunaxa people crossed the Continental Divide for bison hunts. Lower Ktunaxa people built their economy on fishing. David Thompson of the North West Company explored the Columbia and Kootenay river system and built a post near present-day Invermere in 1807, among the first European incursions into the territory, not the last.

## Wild Horse Creek and Galbraith's Ferry

Gold turned up at Wild Horse Creek in 1863, and American and British prospectors crossed into Ktunaxa territory without treaty or agreement. John Galbraith answered the traffic in 1864 by running a ferry across the Kootenay River at the crossing point miners used, and the settlement that grew up around it took his name: Galbraith's Ferry.

## The Jailbreak

In 1884, Indian Reserve Commissioner Peter O'Reilly drew the local reserve boundary and left out Joseph's Prairie, land near present-day Cranbrook that Isadore's people had farmed for years and depended on. Provincial commissioners reconfirmed O'Reilly's line against Isadore's objection in September 1887. Three months earlier, in May 1887, Isadore had led the forcible jailbreak of a man named Kapula, held on thin evidence in connection with the deaths of miners. That jailbreak, not vague "lawless conditions," is what brought the Mounted Police to this bluff.

## Isadore

The Dictionary of Canadian Biography, drawing on Sam Steele's own account, calls Isadore the most influential Indigenous leader Steele encountered in his career. Steele ranked him above Plains chiefs like Crowfoot or Red Crow, names that usually take up more space in Mounted Police history. Facing Steele's armed detachment in August 1887, Isadore judged resistance futile and did not fight. He moved to allotted land along the Kootenay River, at what is now ʔaq'am, and went back to farming. He died of influenza in the winter of 1893-94.

## Kootenay Post to Fort Steele

Superintendent Samuel Steele arrived in August 1887 with about 75 North-West Mounted Police officers and built a post on this bluff, first called Kootenay Post — the first NWMP post west of the Rocky Mountains. Steele negotiated Kapula's formal release that same month, after investigating the case himself. The detachment left in the summer of 1888, transferred back to Fort Macleod, after roughly a year here. Local residents petitioned to rename the settlement in Steele's honour, and Galbraith's Ferry became Fort Steele in 1888.

## Boom and Bypass

The Crow's Nest railway approach and a run of silver-lead strikes in the early 1890s built Fort Steele into a boom town. It didn't last. In 1898 the Canadian Pacific Railway routed its British Columbia Southern line through Cranbrook instead, and Fort Steele's businesses and residents went with it.

## What's Preserved

The province bought the townsite in 1961 and began restoring it — though the Canadian Encyclopedia gives 1963, and the two sources don't agree; "early 1960s" is the safer read. On October 20, 1988, an Order-in-Council reclassified the site from Provincial Park to Provincial Heritage Property. More than 60 original and recreated buildings stand today, but the original 1887 fort itself is not one of them. No NWMP structures survive. The actual fort site, designated a National Historic Site of Canada on May 15, 1925, is marked only by a stockade footprint and a cairn. The one physical link to 1864 is the Galbraith's Ferry Office building, the sole survivor of the original crossing settlement.

## Four Ktunaxa Communities, One Ongoing Negotiation

Four Ktunaxa communities make up the Ktunaxa Nation in British Columbia today: ʔaq'am, ʔakisq̓nuk, Yaqan Nuʔkiy, and Ɂakan̓uxunik̓ (Tobacco Plains). The Ktunaxa Nation is currently in treaty negotiations with Canada and British Columbia, more than a century after Peter O'Reilly drew the reserve boundary that pushed Isadore's people off Joseph's Prairie.

You are still standing on the bluff Galbraith picked for a ferry landing in 1864. Three names, one river crossing, and the same 1884 boundary the Ktunaxa Nation and Canada are still negotiating today.
