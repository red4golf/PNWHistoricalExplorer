# Fort Nisqually: Gateway to the Pacific Northwest

The Archaeological Conservancy calls Fort Nisqually the first permanent European settlement on Puget Sound. Despite its name, it was never a military fort — it was a fur-trading and farming post built and run by the Hudson's Bay Company.

## Founding and Early Years

Chief Trader Archibald McDonald established Fort Nisqually in 1833 near Sequalitchew Creek, in what is now DuPont, naming it for the Nisqually people who lived in the area. Dr. William Fraser Tolmie, a young Scottish physician and naturalist, arrived with McDonald's party on May 30, 1833, and stayed about six months before departing that December. In 1843 the company relocated the post to a better-watered site, and that May, Tolmie returned to take charge of the fort, a post he would hold for the next 16 years. He was promoted to Chief Factor in 1855 and left in 1859 to run the Puget Sound Agricultural Company's operations on Vancouver Island.

## An Agricultural and Trading Empire

By 1839, declining fur supplies had pushed the Hudson's Bay Company to establish the Puget Sound Agricultural Company, a subsidiary that turned Fort Nisqually's surrounding land toward livestock and farming. By 1845 the operation ran 2,280 cattle and 5,872 sheep. The fort's daily operations were directed regionally from Fort Vancouver on the Columbia River, headquarters for the Hudson's Bay Company's Pacific Northwest holdings.

The people who worked at the fort came from many places. Its crews included Englishmen, Americans, Iroquois, Scotsmen, French-Canadians, and Hawaiians the British called "Kanakas." Tolmie himself arrived with four Hawaiian paddlers, part of a broader pattern of Hawaiian labor in the company's Pacific Northwest posts — in 1825, 18 of the 37 men stationed at Fort Vancouver were Hawaiian. Native American employees, primarily Nisqually and Puyallup people, served the fort as guides, interpreters, and herders, and the surrounding Coast Salish communities were its trading partners.

## Shifting Borders

The Oregon Treaty of 1846 set the U.S.-British boundary at the 49th parallel, placing Fort Nisqually inside American territory. British operations continued at the post regardless.

In May 1849, the fort came under attack — an incident that, combined with the need to assert U.S. territorial claims against Britain, led the U.S. Army to found Fort Steilacoom on August 22, 1849, nearby. The Army leased the Fort Steilacoom site from the Hudson's Bay Company.

In 1869 the Hudson's Bay Company and the Puget Sound Agricultural Company sold their remaining claims to the United States government. The land was later homesteaded by a former Hudson's Bay Company clerk who became a U.S. citizen.

## Reconstruction in Point Defiance Park

In the 1930s, the Works Progress Administration rebuilt Fort Nisqually at its current site in Tacoma's Point Defiance Park, away from the original ground in DuPont. Two original structures from the fort's Hudson's Bay Company era — the Factor's House and the Granary — were preserved and moved here, and stand today among the WPA-era reconstruction.

## Visiting Today

Costumed interpreters recreate daily life at the fort as it would have looked in 1855, demonstrating trades including blacksmithing, candle making, and woodworking across the fort's grounds.
