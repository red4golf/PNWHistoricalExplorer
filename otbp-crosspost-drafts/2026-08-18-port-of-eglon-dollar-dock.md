Drive north from Kingston on the east side of the Kitsap Peninsula and you'll pass Eglon without much warning — a scatter of houses above a beach, three miles short of Point No Point. There's no sign announcing what happened here, because nothing announces it. But on August 30, 1919, a bill of sale changed hands in this small settlement for exactly one dollar, and the wooden dock it transferred became the reason for one of the stubbornest little governments in Washington State.

**A Name That Was Second Choice**

Families started settling here in the early 1900s, along a small creek named for homesteaders Charles and Jessie Silver. They called the place Silver Creek, and it might have stayed Silver Creek if the name hadn't already been taken by another Washington community when residents applied for their own post office in 1906. They needed something else. They chose Eglon, out of the Old Testament — the story goes it won because it was the shortest of three names on the list.

**Getting Here Meant Getting Wet**

Before there was a dock, there was rowing. Mosquito Fleet steamers passed the Eglon beach several times a day on their runs between Seattle and the small towns around the sound, but with nothing to tie up to, someone had to row out and meet them. In decent weather that worked well enough to move a passenger or some light cargo. In bad weather, you stayed aboard and tried again the next day. Livestock didn't get the rowboat treatment — they were swum ashore, or herded up the beach from Kingston on foot.

Residents built their first real dock in 1913, through the Eglon Dock Company. It didn't last: by 1918, upkeep had fallen to too few volunteers, the structure was unsafe, and the community tore it down to start over. A private Community Dock Company put up a replacement that same fall, and the Puget Sound Navigation Company announced that starting November 10, 1918, "the Steamer PUGET will land at the new dock in Eglon in both directions."

**Eleven Voters and a Dollar**

Even before the new dock was built, the community had turned to the harder question of how to keep one maintained. On July 12, 1919, eleven registered voters turned out to decide whether to form a public port district — and ten said yes. Gust Sevon, Nikolai Naykki, and Henry Johnson became the first port commissioners. The county canvassed the vote on July 21, and about six weeks later, on August 30, the dock passed to the new Port of Eglon for that famous single dollar. Naykki took the paid job of wharfinger, dock manager — though the position came with a catch: out of his own salary, he had to buy the coal oil that kept the dock's night light burning.

**The Dock Disappeared. The Port Didn't.**

Progress caught up with Eglon fast. The first car ferry ran from Kingston in 1923. The county highway reached the community in 1924. Scheduled boat service to the dock ended by 1933, and the dock itself came down by the early 1940s. By 1940 the port had even stopped collecting taxes — its last real expenditure for years was fifty dollars spent on lumber for a beach bathhouse in 1945.

What the Port of Eglon would not do, through any of this, was dissolve.

In 1960, the state legislature floated a plan to fold Washington's many small port districts into one statewide authority. Port of Eglon commissioner George Coles put his community's position plainly: "We don't want any industry," he said. "Leave us alone. We don't want to be infringed upon. We want to keep our little bit of beach and use it for the children to swim." At the time, Kitsap County had twenty-one separate port districts — more than any other county in the state. By 2011 that number had shrunk to twelve through voluntary consolidations and dissolutions, and Kitsap still held the record.

The statewide consolidation plan died. Eglon's port did not.

**What's Left**

Go north from Kingston today and you can still park above the beach, launch a boat for free, and sit at a picnic table beside a fire pit on the hundred feet of shoreline the port kept after the steamers stopped calling. Neighbors still gather there on Wednesday nights all through the summer. It isn't much of a port, in the sense that word usually means — no industry, no development, no growth. That was always exactly the point.

**If You Go**

- **Port of Eglon boat launch**, Eglon (north of Kingston). Free boat launch, a parking lot, and a picnic area with tables, a picnic shelter, and a fire pit.
- The Wednesday evening gatherings through the summer are a longstanding neighborhood tradition — a good reason to time a visit for one, but worth remembering it's a community gathering place, not a public event.
- There's no dock left to see — that's the point of the story. What you're visiting is the hundred feet of beach the port fought to keep instead.

Another quiet corner of the Northwest, off the beaten path.
