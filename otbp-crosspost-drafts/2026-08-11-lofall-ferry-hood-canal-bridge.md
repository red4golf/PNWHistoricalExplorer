On August 12, 1961, thousands of people sat in a five-mile traffic backup on Hood Canal, waiting to drive across a brand-new bridge that was opening fifteen months behind schedule. The floating section ran 6,520 feet across 23 concrete pontoons — the longest bridge of its kind in the world, and the first one anyone had tried to float on salt water with tides that rise and fall dramatically over the course of a day.

At the end of a road called Wesley Way sits a quiet private dock. That little beach landing has carried the state's ferry traffic in three separate stretches — a regular run in the 1950s, then twice more when the bridge was out — and it started out as one fisherman's side project.

**A Fisherman's Dock**

Bill Lofall was a commercial fisherman. He and his wife Martha owned a stretch of Hood Canal beach property, and in 1949 the Black Ball Line — a private ferry operator — asked to put a landing on it. Bill built the dock and the access road, which still carries the name Wesley Way. Black Ball supplied the pilings and mechanical gear. The landing was commissioned on June 10, 1950, and within a year the state had taken over Black Ball's Puget Sound ferry routes entirely.

**Three Buildings, One Crossing**

The crossing needed more than a dock. A ferry waiting room went up, with a single office inside. A bait house let Bill and Martha sell herring to the local fishing fleet. And Bill barged in a building from Scandia and set it up as a restaurant, the Sea Breeze Inn, where Ruth Ryan sold hot food to travelers waiting for the next boat. Her husband Red ran the tavern and gas station at the Lofall four corners nearby.

**The Boats That Came and Went**

Four vessels worked the run over its eleven years. The wooden ferry Kitsap, good for about 30 cars, stayed on the route the entire time. The wooden Vashon made 28 crossings a day until 1954. The Olympic filled in for about a month, from March to April 1954. Then came the Rhododendron — originally the Gov. Herbert R. O'Conner on Chesapeake Bay, retired there when a bridge put her out of work, then bought by Washington State, towed through the Panama Canal, and renamed for the state flower. The crossing itself took about ten minutes.

**Retired, Then Recommissioned — Twice**

When the Hood Canal Bridge opened in 1961, the ferry run was retired and the Lofalls got their property back. The old waiting room and the Sea Breeze Inn became rental houses. In 1973, the Lofall Community Corporation was formed to buy the site.

Six years after that, on the morning of February 13, 1979, the western half of the Hood Canal Bridge broke loose in a windstorm and sank. The state came looking for Lofall again. WSDOT built a long pier for a passenger-only run to South Point on the Jefferson County side, then modified the main dock to land car ferries. The rebuilt bridge reopened on October 3, 1982. Then in 2009, when the bridge's aging east half was replaced and the bridge closed for six weeks, the state came back a third time: a passenger water shuttle ran between the Lofall dock and South Point, with buses to park-and-ride lots on each side.

**What's There Now**

The dock itself is private property. If you want a public place to stop, Salsbury Point Park, a Kitsap County park one mile west of Port Gamble, has its own dock in the water from April to October.

There's something almost funny about it, if you squint: the state builds an enormous, record-setting bridge that makes a little beach ferry obsolete, and then spends the next several decades occasionally needing that same beach back. Hood Canal has a way of humbling infrastructure. The dock at Lofall just happened to be standing there each time it did.

**If You Go**

- **Wesley Way, Lofall** — the access road Bill Lofall built still runs down to the dock. The dock is private property.
- **Salsbury Point Park** — 3160 NW Wheeler Street, Poulsbo, one mile west of Port Gamble. Kitsap County park; its dock is in from April to October.
- The Hood Canal Bridge carries SR 104 across the canal and is a straightforward stop if you're already touring North Kitsap.

Another quiet corner of the Northwest, off the beaten path.
