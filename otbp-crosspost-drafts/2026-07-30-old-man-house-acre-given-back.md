Cross the Agate Passage bridge onto the Kitsap Peninsula and you're a short drive from a one-acre beach park that doesn't look like much. No plaque towering over the parking lot, no gift shop. But that acre carries one of the more difficult, and more instructive, stories in Puget Sound history — one of erasure, and then, remarkably, of return.

**A Longhouse Big Enough to Hold a Village**

Old Man House stood on the beach at Suquamish, directly across Agate Passage from the north end of Bainbridge Island. It was the winter village of the Suquamish and the home of both Chief Seattle and Chief Kitsap. Accounts of its exact size differ — HistoryLink puts it at roughly 900 feet long and 60 feet wide, divided into 40 apartments, while the Suquamish Museum simply calls it the largest longhouse in the region. Either way, this wasn't a single family's home. It was a center of communal life.

In 1870, federal officials ordered it destroyed. The official reason given was public health. The actual effect was assimilation — communal living was exactly what federal policy of that era was designed to break apart, and tearing down the house did it.

You should sit with that for a moment before moving on. It's the hinge the rest of this story turns on.

**Leaving the Land Without Leaving It**

The Suquamish kept living on the ground where Old Man House had stood until 1904, when they were pushed onto individual family allotments. That same year, poor and squeezed, the tribe sold the beach itself to the U.S. Army. Developers owned it after that. In 1950 it became Old Man House State Park — one acre of shell-strewn sand, all that remained in public memory of what had once stood there. Park signs updated in 1975 described the Suquamish as people who "once lived in the area."

They had never left.

**A Vote, and a Recess**

On August 12, 2004, the Washington State Parks and Recreation Commission voted to give that one acre to the Suquamish Tribe. It was the first time the state had ever given land to a tribe. The seven-member commission's decision was unanimous, though it came with conditions: the tribe agreed to spend $50,000 over five years on maintenance, and the state kept the right to take the park back if the land were ever privatized or the park's hours and other conditions ignored.

Not everyone in town wanted it to happen. One neighborhood group fought the transfer; another worked alongside the tribe to see it through. Reverend Tom Thresher of the Suquamish Community Congregational United Church of Christ told the commission, "You have an opportunity to heal old wounds, and I think reach out a hand in friendship today that I think will heal our community." Rob Purser, the tribal member who led the effort, called it "a good step in the healing process for the tribe."

When the vote came down, the commission had to recess. Tribal members sang a song of thanks and drummed. About twenty Suquamish who had arrived by canoe beat their paddles on the floor.

**What's There Now**

Old Man House Park is still there, at 17818 South Angeline Avenue NE in Suquamish — one acre of beach, returned. Nearby stands the House of Awakened Culture, dedicated in February 2009 and built to honor the longhouse that had been destroyed. It was the first traditional communal space the Suquamish had had in 139 years. Chief Seattle's grave is in the Suquamish cemetery, where a graveside ceremony each August anchors Chief Seattle Days, held in town since 1911.

**If You Go**

Old Man House Park sits at 17818 South Angeline Avenue NE, Suquamish, WA — near the House of Awakened Culture and Chief Seattle's grave in the Suquamish cemetery. Treat it as the tribe's living history that it is: a place to walk respectfully, not a roadside attraction. Chief Seattle Days, held in town each August, is the most visible time to understand what this ground means to the people who fought to get it back.

Another quiet corner of the Northwest, off the beaten path.
