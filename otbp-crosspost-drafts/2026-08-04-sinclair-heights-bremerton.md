Much of the hill west of downtown Bremerton is car dealerships today. But for about five years in the 1940s, this hilltop held a crowded wartime neighborhood that mattered far more than its size — and it produced Quincy Jones.

**A City That Outgrew Itself**

Bremerton's 1940 census counted seven Black residents. By 1945 it had more than 4,500. The pull was the Puget Sound Naval Shipyard, ramping up after Pearl Harbor and desperate for workers. Federal policy did not require segregated housing, and the nine developments built around Bremerton and Port Orchard to handle the influx were planned as integrated. Then some white residents in those developments objected to Black neighbors, and the Bremerton Housing Authority made one project the de facto development for Black families and assigned most new arrivals there: Sinclair Park, 280 prefabricated units spread across 80 acres on a hill west of Bremerton, named for nearby Sinclair Inlet. People called it Sinclair Heights.

By August 1944, the housing authority's own count showed 342 Black families in its care. Two hundred seventy-nine of them lived in Sinclair Park. Of the development's 280 units, 279 held Black families. The last one held a Filipino family.

**Life on the Hill**

It was not an easy place to live. Construction started in the summer of 1942, and the first residents — Eugene Aires and Elwood Greer — moved in that March of 1943. There was no landscaping. There were no streetlights at first, and longtime residents remembered the darkness for decades afterward. The neighborhood sat a mile outside the school district, far enough that the bus wouldn't come up the hill. Until a phone booth was added, the development had exactly one telephone, and it was in the community center.

That community center is where this story turns. Built in 1943 for about $57,000, it was one of the first buildings designed by a young Seattle architecture firm called Naramore, Bain, Brady and Johanson — the firm that would later become NBBJ, one of the largest architecture practices in the world. Inside, volunteers ran a night library twice a week. Voting booths went up there at election time. Lilian Walker started Bremerton's NAACP branch inside its walls. A drama club staged a production called "Jiving Macbeth" on its small stage.

**The Boy and the Piano**

On that stage sat an upright piano. An eleven-year-old boy broke into the building with some friends to steal a pie, wandered into the recreation room, and found it waiting for him.

"I went up there, paused, stared, and then tinkled on it for a moment," he wrote years later. "That's where I began to find peace."

The boy was Quincy Jones. He kept sneaking back until a sympathetic supervisor started leaving the door unlocked for him.

It is worth sitting with that for a second. One of the most influential musicians of the twentieth century found his way to a piano because he'd broken into a segregated wartime community center to steal food. That's rather the point of a neighborhood like this one — it held whole lives inside a system that sorted them onto one hill, and sometimes those lives went on to reshape American music.

**Built to Disappear**

Sinclair Heights was never meant to last. The houses were built from panels that were easy to take down. Eighty units went up for sale on July 1, 1946, and by 1948 the rest were gone — some of them floated down to waterfront lots around the Kitsap Peninsula and turned into summer cabins. What remained was streets, sidewalks, and the community center, which went on to serve as a National Guard armory until it was torn down in late 2002.

**What's Left**

Much of the hilltop is car dealerships today; the community center's footprint is now an emergency-services training center. But the story has found its way back downtown. Quincy Square, with a woven bronze sculpture of Jones's face, opened on Fourth Street on July 12, 2025, three blocks up from the Bremerton ferry terminal; interpretive signs on Jones's early life and Sinclair Heights followed. And in the lobby of the Kitsap History Museum stands a scuffed Melville Clark upright piano, said to be the one from the hill. Nobody has roped it off. You can put your hands on the keys.

A neighborhood built to be temporary, and torn down on schedule, still managed to leave something behind that a city built a square around. That is the strange arithmetic of a place like Sinclair Heights: designed to disappear, and it mostly did, except for the one thing that mattered most.

**If You Go**

- **Quincy Square** — Fourth Street, downtown Bremerton, a few blocks from the Bremerton ferry terminal. Look for the woven bronze sculpture of Jones's face and the interpretive signs on his early life and Sinclair Heights.
- **Kitsap History Museum** — downtown Bremerton. The piano attributed to the Sinclair Park community center stands in the lobby, unroped. Check current hours before you go.
- The original hilltop site, west of downtown, is now largely car dealerships; the community center's footprint is an emergency-services training center.

This is sensitive ground — a story about segregation carried out by a local housing authority's assignments rather than by law, in a city that badly needed the workers it was mistreating. It deserves to be told plainly, without smoothing over the parts that are hard to sit with. That the story ends with a sculpture doesn't undo what came before it.

Another quiet corner of the Northwest, off the beaten path.
