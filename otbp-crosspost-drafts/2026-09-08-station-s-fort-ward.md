Walk the shoreline at Fort Ward State Park today and it's easy to miss what happened here. The former Post Exchange that housed Station S still stands, but little about it announces what happened inside. At 1:28 in the morning on December 7, 1941, Navy radiomen at a secret station here intercepted a message from Tokyo to the Japanese embassy in Washington, D.C. — an order to break off peace talks with the United States. Its real purpose was to signal that Japan was about to strike Pearl Harbor. Almost no one on the island knew the station existed at all.

**A Listening Post Looking for Better Reception**

The story doesn't start with the attack — it starts a decade earlier, with a reception problem. In May 1932, the Navy built a listening post at Fort Stevens, near Astoria, Oregon, to monitor Japanese diplomatic radio traffic. By 1938 the signal there had gone bad, and a survey turned up something useful: Bainbridge Island picked up the same transmissions far more cleanly. In September 1939, the operation moved north into Fort Ward, a former Army coast artillery post, setting up inside the old Post Exchange building under the deliberately bland name Communications Support Activities. Rhombic antennas went up across the fort's parade ground. In 1940, the operation got a new name — Station S — and a new job: recording Japan's Tokyo-to-San Francisco diplomatic traffic in Japanese Morse code, and helping triangulate the positions of Japanese ships.

**A Cover Story With a Photo Spread**

Here's the part that makes this a genuinely clever piece of tradecraft. To keep Station S's real work hidden, the Navy opened a Naval Reserve Radio School at Fort Ward on October 28, 1940. Up to forty students at a time spent four months learning Morse code, typing, and elementary seamanship, housed in rehabbed Army barracks. The Seattle Times even ran a full-page photo spread on the school — and that was exactly the point. As far as the public knew, training radiomen was the entire reason the Navy was on Bainbridge Island. The real intercept work went on across the parade ground, inside a converted PX building, while the neighbors assumed they knew the whole story.

**The Message That Signaled War**

In November and early December 1941, Japanese diplomatic message traffic was climbing fast. Station S's commander, B. C. Purrington, flagged a jump in Japanese shipping activity in his reports to the Chief of Naval Operations. Then, in the small hours of December 7, came the message that mattered most: instructions for the Japanese ambassador to break off negotiations, meant to reach the U.S. Secretary of State shortly before the Pearl Harbor attack began. Delays in translation held it up. The attack started at 1:20 p.m. Washington time. The United States declared war the next day.

It's worth sitting with what that means: a message that signaled war passed through a radio receiver on Bainbridge Island, copied down by sailors whose own neighbors thought they were running nothing more than a training school.

**What Came After**

Station S didn't close when the war ended. It kept listening through the Cold War, switching its attention to Russian and then North Korean traffic, until the Navy shut it down in December 1953 and moved the operation to Marietta, Washington. The site was abandoned in 1958, and in 1960, 137 acres of the old fort became Fort Ward State Park — the same shoreline you can walk today.

**If You Go**

- **Fort Ward State Park** — Bainbridge Island. The park occupies the former fort grounds, including the shoreline where Station S once listened for signals out of Tokyo.
- The former Post Exchange that housed Station S still stands, but little about it announces what happened inside, so this is a walk best paired with reading the history beforehand — the significance is in knowing what you're standing on.

This is a story that deserves to be told with some weight to it — it's tied directly to the opening hours of America's war in the Pacific, and to the men and women whose quiet, unglamorous work at a radio console mattered as much as anything happening on a battlefield.

Another quiet corner of the Northwest, off the beaten path.
