Walk into Battle Point Park today and you'll pass through two stone pillars with wartime fencing still attached to them, on your way to a playground, ballfields, and a telescope open to the public. None of it gives away what stood here for most of three decades: the tallest structure on Bainbridge Island, and a secret almost nobody on the island was supposed to know.

**One Last Climb**

In 1972, three men from the Bainbridge Island parks district — director Dennis McCurdy, assistant director Larry Burris, and commissioner Fred Grimm — climbed an 800-foot steel tower at Battle Point and sat down at the top to eat lunch. They knew it was their last chance. The tower was scheduled to come down within days. The climb up took them about 90 minutes. Coming down took half an hour.

**Why the Tower Was There**

The Navy built a radio transmitter station at Battle Point in September 1942, north of Fort Ward. Fort Ward was the secret listening post, intercepting Tokyo's radio traffic; Battle Point's job, per HistoryLink, was relaying messages from the Pacific to the 13th Naval Command in Seattle. The station included a reinforced concrete transmitter building, a helix building, and four 300-foot towers — the 800-footer came later, and was for years the tallest thing on the island. The stone pillars and fencing still standing at the park entrance today were built during the war specifically to keep people away from all of it.

**The Towers Come Down**

The Navy shut Battle Point down in 1971, and the ground passed to the Bainbridge Island Metropolitan Park & Recreation District, which took formal title to more than 90 acres on May 5, 1972. Part of the deal: the towers had to go. Locals had long regarded the big one as a "terrible temptation to climb," and that year 311 tons of steel came down, hauled away by a contractor who got to keep the scrap in exchange for the work. The 864th Engineer Battalion, out of Fort Lewis, then graded the site as a training exercise — work that saved the parks district an estimated $100,000.

The buildings, unlike the towers, were left standing.

**From Helix House to Observatory**

In 1992, Edwin Ritchie, John Rudolph, and Mac Gardiner founded the Battle Point Astronomical Association and turned their attention to the abandoned Helix House — a 26-by-38-foot building with 25-foot walls that had once housed the coil generating the long carrier waves sent out to the fleet. The parks district was planning to tear it down. The new association talked the district out of it.

Gardiner, a retired Boeing engineer, tracked down a 27.5-inch Zerodur mirror, surplus from a discontinued Strategic Defense Initiative program. Rudolph, an architect, drew up the renovation plans and ran the volunteer construction crew. Ritchie, a machinist and prosthetic-limb designer by trade, machined nearly all of the telescope himself, bending its steel fork mount into shape with a 20-ton press. He died on March 13, 1997, not long after finishing the work. The Edwin E. Ritchie Observatory was dedicated that December 21.

**What's There Now**

Visit Battle Point Park at 10800 Battle Point Drive NE and the war is still visible if you know where to look. The old transmitter building now hosts gymnastics classes. The Helix House holds the largest telescope open to the public in the Pacific Northwest, under a 17-foot plywood dome the volunteers built themselves. The Battle Point Astronomical Association opens the observatory to visitors on Fridays and Saturdays, and runs planetarium shows on Saturdays — check the BPAA events calendar for what's coming up.

**If You Go**

- **Battle Point Park** — 10800 Battle Point Drive NE, Bainbridge Island, WA 98110. Free public park with trails, ballfields, a playground, and pond.
- **Edwin E. Ritchie Observatory**, run by the Battle Point Astronomical Association, sits inside the old Navy Helix House on the park grounds — the BPAA lists the observatory as open to visitors Fridays and Saturdays, 11 a.m. to 3 p.m., with Saturday planetarium shows at 12:30 and 2:30 p.m. Check the events calendar before visiting for updates and open dates.
- Look for the original wartime entrance pillars and fencing near the park entrance — one of the few physical traces the Navy left behind.

There's something worth sitting with in how completely this place changed hands: from the most secretive spot on the island to one of its most public, from a tower nobody could climb without permission to a telescope anyone can look through. The tower is gone. The view from the same ground is still there for whoever wants it.

Another quiet corner of the Northwest, off the beaten path.
