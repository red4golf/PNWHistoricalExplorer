Drive up the Kitsap Peninsula toward Hood Canal and you'll pass through a town that looks like someone airlifted a slice of coastal New England onto Puget Sound and forgot to change it back. That's not an accident. Port Gamble was built by New Englanders, for New Englanders, and for 142 years it did one thing without stopping: cut lumber.

The town's whole existence traces back to a captain who almost built it somewhere else.

**A Long Way to Find a Sandspit**

In July 1853, William C. Talbot anchored the schooner Julius Pringle in Discovery Bay and set out in a small sailboat to explore Hood Canal, hunting for a place to put a sawmill. Getting there had not been quick. Talbot had captained the 50-ton Julius Pringle from his hometown of East Machias, Maine, all the way around Cape Horn to Puget Sound — a voyage of 171 days.

His first choice was Port Ludlow. It had everything he wanted, and someone else had already claimed it. So he kept looking, and found a sandspit at the mouth of a two-mile bay: sheltered from the prevailing winds, ringed with Douglas fir, with room enough for a mill and the buildings around it. Good enough to become, almost by default, the site of the longest continuously running sawmill in American history.

**A Place That Already Had a Name**

The spit wasn't empty when Talbot found it. S'Klallam families lived there and on the bluff above, and they called the place Teekalet — brightness of the noonday sun. Talbot borrowed the name for his mill. His partner, Josiah Keller, later persuaded the S'Klallam to move across the bay to Point Julia, in exchange for free lumber, firewood, and Christmas gifts. Many of them went to work at the mill, commuting to the job site by canoe.

**Building It From Scratch**

Talbot landed ten men, most of them from East Machias, and they threw up a bunkhouse, a cookhouse, and a store before touching the mill itself. The actual machinery arrived separately, aboard the clipper schooner L.P. Foster, along with Keller, who took charge of the operation once he landed. Within a week of getting the engine, boilers, and a single-blade muley saw ashore, the crew had steam up and cut their first log — by September 1853. Early output ran around 2,000 board feet a day, roughly a tenth of what a five-room house of the era required.

Here's the detail worth holding onto: Talbot's very first Puget Sound lumber shipment didn't come from his own mill at all. That same September, before his mill's machinery had even arrived, he bought pilings from Henry Yesler in Seattle.

**A Company Town, Paid in Fifty-Cent Pieces**

The town that grew up on the spit was pure transplanted New England, right down to its habits. Workers earned about $30 a month, paid so reliably in fifty-cent pieces that "four bit" coins became a kind of badge of Port Gamble employment. In 1859 the company built the first school in Kitsap County. The settlement itself didn't take the name Port Gamble until 1868, honoring a Navy lieutenant wounded in the War of 1812.

**The Mill That Didn't Stop**

The mill's last 20 workers reported for their final day on November 30, 1995 — 142 continuous years after that first cut, and the longest run of any sawmill in the United States.

**If You Go**

The Port Gamble townsite sits on the Kitsap Peninsula at the mouth of Hood Canal and has been on the National Register of Historic Places since 1966. You can still walk the whole story: the general store, the 1879 church, and the Walker-Ames House up on the bluff all survived the mill's closing. Across the bay at Point Julia, the Port Gamble S'Klallam Tribe lives on the water its ancestors never really left.

Another quiet corner of the Northwest, off the beaten path.
