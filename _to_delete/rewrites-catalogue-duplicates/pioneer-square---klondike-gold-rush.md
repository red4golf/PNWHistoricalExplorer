## Facts corrected

1. **(implied) the city / mayor promoted Seattle as the 'Gateway to the Klondike'**
   - Problem: The campaign was run by Erastus Brainerd through the Chamber of Commerce, not by the mayor. Mayor William D. Wood's famous act was resigning mid-term (by telegram from a San Francisco convention) to chase gold himself.
   - Corrected to: Erastus Brainerd ran the 'Gateway to the Klondike' campaign; Mayor W.D. Wood separately resigned to chase gold himself.
   - Source: UW CSPN; Archives West; HistoryLink File 687 *(editorial-secondary)*
2. **with its 'ton of gold'**
   - Problem: The P-I's actual extra-edition headline was 'GOLD! GOLD! GOLD! GOLD!'; the text reported 'more than a ton of gold'; real cargo was ~two tons.
   - Corrected to: Attribute 'ton of gold' as the popular framing derived from the P-I's reporting; the headline itself was 'GOLD! GOLD! GOLD! GOLD!' and cargo was roughly two tons.
   - Source: HistoryLink File 687 *(editorial-secondary)*
3. **Outfitters: Cooper & Levy, Schwabacher Hardware, MacDougall & Southwick**
   - Problem: MacDougall & Southwick was Seattle's earliest department store (name 1891, roots 1874), not a dedicated Klondike outfitter; the rush boosted its general business.
   - Corrected to: Keep Cooper & Levy and Schwabacher as outfitters; describe MacDougall & Southwick as Seattle's earliest department store, which benefited from the boom rather than specializing in outfits.
   - Source: HistoryLink File 22709 *(editorial-secondary)*
4. **Filson (outdoor gear) got its start**
   - Problem: Founding year (1897) and outfitting purpose correct, but the original business was named 'Seattle Woolen Manufacturing Company'; the 'C.C. Filson Co.' name came later.
   - Corrected to: C.C. Filson founded his Seattle outfitting business in 1897, originally trading under the Seattle Woolen Manufacturing name; it later became the C.C. Filson Company.
   - Source: HistoryLink File 11150 *(editorial-secondary)*
5. **population doubled ... grew from 42,000 to 80,000 in a single decade**
   - Problem: Accurate for 1890-1900 (+88%, 'nearly doubled') but easily mis-anchored; the biggest surge came 1900-1910 (to 237,194).
   - Corrected to: Seattle nearly doubled from 42,837 (1890) to 80,671 (1900), then nearly tripled to 237,194 by 1910.
   - Source: U.S. Census via HistoryLink File 9332 *(primary-official)*

## Claims that could not be sourced, and were dropped

- Booster superlatives with no attributable source ('Seattle struck it rich,' 'the city that gold built,' 'boomtown magic') - drop or attribute.

- Framing the mayor as the promotional mastermind - unsupported.
- Unattributed significance claims ('the rush made Seattle great') - attribute, don't state as fact.
- Purely celebratory tone that omits documented harm to northern Indigenous peoples.

## New material found in research

- The scoop mechanics: reporter Beriah Brown Jr. tugged out to the Portland and wired the story before it docked.
- Mayor W.D. Wood resigning by telegram from a San Francisco convention to chase gold himself.
- Cooper & Levy's outfits stacked on the sidewalk and the ad line 'You are going to a country where grub is more valuable than gold.'
- The North-West Mounted Police one-ton / year's-supplies rule enforced at the passes - explains WHY Seattle's outfitting trade boomed.
- Brainerd/Chamber of Commerce Bureau of Information capturing ~3/4 of the outfitting trade - the real engine of the boom.
- Nordstrom's precise origin: ~$13,000 from a sold Klondike claim seeding Wallin & Nordstrom (1901) with Carl Wallin at 4th & Pike.
- Indigenous impact for neutrality: disease among the Tanana and displacement of Native communities in the north.
