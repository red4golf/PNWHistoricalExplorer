## New material found in research

- Billy Barker's claim struck gold on 17 Aug. 1862 at a depth the DCB gives as 40 feet (Geoscience Canada's peer-reviewed figure of ~10-15 m / 33-49 ft corroborates this range); barkerville.ca separately states 52 feet -- flag the conflict rather than picking one silently.
- The original 1861 discovery on Williams Creek that touched off the rush was made not by Barker but by William 'Dutch Bill' Dietz, above Richfield Canyon, at shallow depth -- Barker's claim, staked lower down the creek, was the one that proved deep gravels could pay (Geoscience Canada, peer-reviewed).
- Fanny Bendixen was born c. 1820 in France; she married Louis A. Bendixen in California and the couple opened the St George Hotel on View Street, Victoria, in October 1862 before she struck out alone for Barkerville in summer 1865 (DCB).
- Bendixen opened the Parlour saloon in 1866 and the Bella Union in June 1867, advertised as 'fitted up in the most elegant style' and stocking only the 'best brands of liquors and cigars' (DCB, quoting period advertising).
- The Bella Union burned in the September 1868 fire; Bendixen's losses were estimated at $5,000 (DCB).
- After the fire Bendixen moved her operations to Lightning Creek (a saloon at Van Winkle with 'one of the finest reading rooms and picture galleries on the creek') and then to Stanley, before returning to Barkerville by the late 1870s, where she was 'the only woman to be regularly listed in the business directory' (DCB).
- In 1889, on his last visit to the Cariboo, judge Sir Matthew Baillie Begbie found Bendixen 'in great form; indeed enormous, ... though she was always of goodly diameter' (DCB, quoting Begbie).
- Bendixen died 2 May 1899; her will left her business and two lots to notary Hugh Cochrane, but a codicil bequeathing her earrings to her grandniece instead of her friend Mrs Andrew Kelly was later overturned by a court as signed while Bendixen was 'almost comatose' and 'not competent' (DCB).
- Billy Barker's earlier life: baptized 7 June 1817 in March, Cambridgeshire; married Jane Lavender in 1839 and had a daughter, Emma Eliza, born 1840; abandoned this family when railway expansion displaced canal workers in the mid-1840s and he left for America; his first wife died in a workhouse in 1850. He died 11 July 1894 in Victoria's Old Men's Home of jaw cancer and was buried in an unmarked pauper's grave at Ross Bay Cemetery (DCB).
- The Chee Kung Tong Building was constructed 1874-1877 (with a kitchen lean-to added c. 1883-1885 and a north expansion c. 1905) and was designated a National Historic Site of Canada on 11 April 2008 (Parks Canada).

## Verifier objections remaining (final round, before hand-fix)

- The Begbie quotation is altered from the pack's exact wording. Pack: "'in great form; indeed enormous, ... though she was always of goodly diameter'" (DCB, quoting Begbie, describing him as 'Sir Matthew Baillie Begbie'). Entry: "'in great form, indeed enormous, though she was always of goodly diameter.'" — semicolon changed to comma, the ellipsis marking omitted original text is silently dropped (making it look like a complete, unedited quotation when it is not), and 'Sir' is dropped from Begbie's title without any indication of alteration. A direct quotation should reproduce the pack's wording and punctuation, or mark omissions with an ellipsis.
- 'on gravel other miners had passed over as too deep to bother with' drifts from the pack's corrected guidance, which characterizes the passed-over ground as 'unlikely to hold gold,' not specifically 'too deep.' The entry substitutes a specific causal reason (depth) that isn't the one the source (Geoscience Canada via the pack's correction note) actually gives.
- 'one of the institutions that kept the isolated camp connected to the outside world' (about the Cariboo Sentinel) is an interpretive claim about the newspaper's function that has no direct support in the pack; the pack confirms only the paper's run dates (1865-1874, per DCB bibliography), not this characterization of its role.
