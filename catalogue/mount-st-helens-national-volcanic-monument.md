1. **a wall of debris buried him and his lodge under 150 feet of rock and ash**
   - Problem: No graded source gives a specific 150-foot figure; the documented depth is looser and larger.
   - Corrected to: HistoryLink documents that the blast and avalanche buried Truman's lodge and cabins under "hundreds of feet" of rock, earth, ice, and mud — no specific footage is established in any graded source.
   - Source: HistoryLink.org, "Truman, Harry R. (1896-1980)" (File 22801) *(editorial-secondary)*
2. **"The mountain's part of Truman and Truman's part of that mountain," he told reporters.**
   - Problem: This exact quotation does not appear in HistoryLink's documented record of Truman's statements to press, and no other graded source was found to confirm it.
   - Corrected to: Documented Truman quotes instead include: "The mountain will never hurt me. When you live someplace for 50 years, you either know your country or you're stupid" and "I've made up my mind more than ever not to leave."
   - Source: HistoryLink.org, "Truman, Harry R. (1896-1980)" (File 22801) *(editorial-secondary)*
3. **The sound of the blast was heard in Montana, 1,200 miles north.**
   - Problem: HistoryLink documents the blast's sound being heard much farther away, in a different direction, and does not support the Montana/1,200-miles figure.
   - Corrected to: The explosion's sound was heard as far as Saskatchewan, Canada; it took about an hour to reach King County, Washington, where residents described hearing distant artillery-like "whumps."
   - Source: HistoryLink.org, "Mount St. Helens erupts on May 18, 1980" (File 5457) *(editorial-secondary)*
4. **Lahars, rivers of volcanic mud moving at 50 miles per hour, scoured river valleys for 50 m**
   - Problem: Documented lahar speed and distance figures for the actual 1980 event are lower and directional, not omnidirectional.
   - Corrected to: The Toutle River lahar moved at 10 to 25 mph and traveled about 75 miles down the Toutle-Cowlitz system to reach the Columbia River within 18 hours, temporarily reducing the Columbia's shipping channel depth there from 40 feet to 14 feet.
   - Source: HistoryLink.org, "Mount St. Helens erupts on May 18, 1980" (File 5457) *(editorial-secondary)*
5. **Volcanologist David Johnston was monitoring the eruption from an observation post on Coldw**
   - Problem: The post's formal name and precise distance differ from the entry's description.
   - Corrected to: Johnston's post was named Coldwater II, located 5.7 miles north-northwest of the summit; the ridge was later renamed Johnston Ridge in his honor.
   - Source: USGS Professional Paper 1249, pubs.usgs.gov/pp/1249/report.pdf *(peer-reviewed)*
6. **Ash fell across eleven states and was measurable as far away as Oklahoma.**
   - Problem: No graded source supports eleven states or Oklahoma specifically; USGS's own tephra study measured ashfall only across Washington, Idaho, and western Montana, and USGS's peer-reviewed account of the eruption says ash fell in "troublesome amounts as far east as western Montana."
   - Corrected to: Ash fell in troublesome amounts as far east as western Montana (measured/traceable ashfall documented in Washington, Idaho, and Montana); about 540 million tons of ash were dispersed over more than 22,000 square miles, and the ash cloud circled the globe in 15 days.
   - Source: USGS Professional Paper 1249; USGS Open-File Report 1980-1078, pubs.usgs.gov/of/1980/1078/report.pdf; USGS FAQ "How much ash was there..." *(peer-reviewed)*

## New material found in research

- Monument creation, currently missing from the body text entirely: Mount St. Helens National Volcanic Monument was created by Public Law 97-243, signed by President Reagan on August 26, 1982 — the House passed it 393-8, the Senate by voice vote; principal sponsor was U.S. Rep. Don Bonker (D-WA), with the entire Washington delegation as cosponsors. It was the nation's first National Volcanic Monument, administered by the U.S. Forest Service, comprising ~110,000 acres, with the stated purpose of letting 'geologic forces and ecological succession continue substantially unimpeded.' (govinfo.gov Statutes at Large; HistoryLink File 22951)
- David Johnston's post was formally named Coldwater II, 5.7 miles north-northwest of the summit — later renamed Johnston Ridge in his honor. He attempted a second radio transmission after 'Vancouver, Vancouver, this is it!' but it was garbled by the eruption before contact went silent. (USGS Professional Paper 1249)
- Precise precursor timeline: first substantial seismic sign was a M4.2 earthquake on March 20, 1980 at 3:47 p.m. PST; the first steam-and-ash venting came at 12:36 p.m. PST on March 27, 1980; by late April, survey crews measured the north-flank bulge growing northward at about 5 feet per day. (USGS, 'Reawakening')
- Debris avalanche specifics: ~2.5 cubic kilometers (3.3 billion cubic yards) of material, the largest such landslide in recorded history, traveled roughly 14 miles (23 km) down the North Fork Toutle River valley, filling it to an average depth of about 148 feet in roughly ten minutes, with deposits as thick as 640 feet in places. (USGS 1980 Cataclysmic Eruption; USGS Volcano Watch)
- Spirit Lake's level rose about 197 feet immediately when landslide debris blocked its outlet, then rose another 54 feet by August 1982 as engineers worked out a permanent drainage solution — Coldwater Lake and Castle Lake were newly formed the same way, by debris damming other tributaries. (USGS Volcano Watch)
- Ashfall by the numbers: about 540 million tons dispersed over more than 22,000 square miles; ash reached the central U.S. within about a day and circled the globe in 15 days. (USGS FAQ, 'How much ash was there...')
- Harry Truman on his life at Spirit Lake, an April 1980 quote: 'I've kind of let the place go to hell since then. Eddie and I had spent 37 years loving, working, fighting, laughing, dancing and really living on our mountain and lake.' (HistoryLink File 22801)
- USGS's own framing of ongoing risk: Mount St. Helens is 'the volcano in the Cascades most likely to erupt again in our lifetimes,' though USGS stresses exact timing can't be forecast decades out. (USGS, 'Future Eruptions of Mount St. Helens')
