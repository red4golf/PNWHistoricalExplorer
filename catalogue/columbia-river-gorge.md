## Facts corrected (found during research)

1. **Approximately 270 volcanic fissures released molten lava across 64,000 square miles**
   - Problem: No USGS or other graded source found gives '270 fissures' or '64,000 square miles.' USGS sources describe linear fissure systems (not a specific enumerated count of 270) and give the affected area as 'over 210,000 km2' (~81,000 sq mi), while another USGS office describes it more loosely as 'thousands of square miles.' The 270-fissure figure could not be confirmed anywhere and should be dropped.
   - Corrected to: Drop the specific fissure count. State only that lava issued from linear fissure systems across eastern Washington, Oregon, and Idaho, covering an area USGS gives as 'over 210,000 km² (about 81,000 sq. mi.)'
   - Source: USGS Cascades Volcano Observatory, 'Columbia River Basalt Group Stretches from Oregon to Idaho' *(primary-official)*
2. **individual flows sometimes traveling 400 miles to reach the Pacific Ocean**
   - Problem: No source gives an exact '400 miles' figure. USGS sources describe flows traveling 'many hundreds of kilometers' or 'hundreds of miles' and reaching the Pacific Ocean, but do not cite a precise 400-mile figure.
   - Corrected to: Some flows traveled hundreds of miles to reach the Pacific Ocean (exact distance not specified in USGS sources); at least 20 flows are documented reaching the coast via the Willamette Valley.
   - Source: USGS Cascades Volcano Observatory; USGS Oregon Water Science Center background page *(primary-official)*
3. **The lava accumulated in layers 2,000-5,000 feet thick**
   - Problem: USGS's Oregon Water Science Center page gives individual flow thickness as 10 to more than 300 feet, and total accumulated thickness of the whole basalt sequence as up to about 15,000 feet in places — figures the draft's '2,000-5,000 feet' range does not match.
   - Corrected to: Individual flows ranged from about 10 to over 300 feet thick; the accumulated basalt sequence reaches as much as roughly 15,000 feet thick in places.
   - Source: USGS Oregon Water Science Center, 'Columbia River Basalt Stratigraphy in the Pacific Northwest' *(primary-official)*
4. **when the largest volcanic eruptions in Earth's recent history occurred... during the Mioce**
   - Problem: USGS dates the CRBG eruptions to roughly 17 to 6 million years ago overall, with the bulk of the volume (93%) erupted in about 1.1 million years, roughly 16.7 to 15.6 million years ago — a narrower and differently-bounded window than the draft's '17-12 Ma.'
   - Corrected to: Eruptions spanned roughly 17 to 6 million years ago, with the great majority of lava volume erupted in about 1.1 million years, around 16.7 to 15.6 million years ago.
   - Source: USGS Cascades Volcano Observatory, 'Columbia River Basalt Group Stretches from Oregon to Idaho' *(primary-official)*
5. **The Gorge's formation began 40-60 million years ago when massive granite uplifts created t**
   - Problem: No graded source (USGS, NPS, or USFS) could be found supporting a '40-60 million years ago... granite uplifts' origin claim for the Columbia Basin's outline.
   - Corrected to: Drop this sentence entirely unless a specific USGS/state geological survey source can be located confirming it.
   - Source: Unverifiable — no matching source found *(primary-official)*
6. **roared through the Gorge about 40 times over 2,500 years**
   - Problem: This significantly understates the count and misstates the duration. USGS's dedicated review of the floods states there were 'dozens—likely more than a hundred' separate floods, with at least ~75 before and 30-or-more after a dated tephra marker; NPS states the ice dam failed 'as many as 100 separate times' between about 15,000 and 13,000 years ago. Both indicate flood activity spanned roughly 3,000-4,000 years, not 2,500, with a count far higher than 40.
   - Corrected to: The floods recurred as many as 100 separate times over a period of roughly 3,000-4,000 years, between about 20,000 and 13,000 years ago.
   - Source: USGS, 'The Missoula and Bonneville floods—A review of ice-age megafloods in the Columbia River basin'; National Park Service, 'Ice Age Floods' *(primary-official)*
7. **For over 13,000 years, Native American peoples have called the Columbia River Gorge home..**
   - Problem: Neither figure matches the available editorial-secondary source. The Oregon Encyclopedia gives archaeological evidence of human occupation at Celilo-area village sites as '11,000+ years' — not the draft's '13,000 years' (Gorge-wide) or '10,000 years' (Celilo-specific), and no source distinguishes a separate, larger figure for the whole Gorge.
   - Corrected to: Archaeological evidence indicates human occupation at village sites near Celilo Falls for more than 11,000 years, according to the Oregon Encyclopedia.
   - Source: The Oregon Encyclopedia, 'Celilo Falls' (attributed) *(editorial-secondary)*
8. **Captain Clark documented the Gorge's "high mountains on each side, containing scattering p**
   - Problem: This is not Clark's actual journal wording. The primary edition (Moulton, UNL) records his October 30, 1805 entry as: 'High Mounts. on each Side, ruged and covd. with a variety of timber Such as Pine Spruce Seder Cotton wood Oake' — different phrasing, spelling, and species list (includes spruce and cedar) than the draft's paraphrase-presented-as-quote.
   - Corrected to: Replace with the verbatim primary-source quote: "High Mounts. on each Side, ruged and covd. with a variety of timber Such as Pine Spruce Seder Cotton wood Oake" (Clark, October 30, 1805).
   - Source: Journals of the Lewis and Clark Expedition, University of Nebraska-Lincoln digital edition (Gary Moulton, ed.) *(peer-reviewed)*
9. **the Corps of Discovery spent 37 days navigating the treacherous Columbia River Gorge**
   - Problem: No graded source located confirms a specific '37 days' figure for the Gorge passage alone.
   - Corrected to: Drop the specific day count, or replace with dated anchor points that are confirmed: they sighted Beacon Rock (near the Gorge's western end) on October 31, 1805, and reached the Pacific Ocean vicinity in mid-November 1805.
   - Source: National Park Service, 'Clark Knew They Were Near Pacific' *(primary-official)*
10. **The completion of Bonneville Dam in 1938 forever changed the river's character, flooding t**
   - Problem: The 'forcing the relocation of Native American communities' claim is unsourced and imprecise. The confirmed, specific fact is different in kind: the federal government promised 50 in-lieu fishing sites to compensate Native fishers for lost fishing grounds, but initially completed only three.
   - Corrected to: Bonneville Dam went into service June 6, 1938 (construction 1933-1938), flooding the Cascades Rapids stretch of the river. The federal government promised 50 in-lieu fishing sites to Native fishers who lost access to fishing grounds, but only three were initially built.
   - Source: The Oregon Encyclopedia, 'Bonneville Dam' (attributed); HistoryLink.org, 'Bonneville Dam officially goes into service on June 6, 1938' (attributed) *(editorial-secondary)*
11. **With over 90 waterfalls on the Oregon side alone, including the iconic Multnomah Falls**
   - Problem: No NPS, USFS, or Oregon State Parks source located confirms a specific count of '90 waterfalls.' This figure could not be independently verified against any graded source.
   - Corrected to: Drop the specific waterfall count unless a Forest Service or state parks source with that figure is located; Multnomah Falls can be retained as a named, well-documented waterfall in the Scenic Area.
   - Source: Unverifiable — no matching graded source found *(primary-official)*

## New material found in research

- Clark's actual verbatim journal words for Oct. 30, 1805 (Moulton/UNL primary edition): "High Mounts. on each Side, ruged and covd. with a variety of timber Such as Pine Spruce Seder Cotton wood Oake" — with landslide evidence noted separately: rocks projecting into the river that "have the appearance of haveing fallen from the highe hills." This replaces the draft's inaccurate paraphrase-as-quote.
- On October 31, 1805 the party sighted Beacon Rock (NPS) and recognized tidal influence, correctly judging they were near the ocean; they reached the Pacific in mid-November 1805 (NPS).
- Bonneville Dam: construction ran 1933–1938 (Oregon Encyclopedia); the dam went into official service June 6, 1938 (HistoryLink); it used one million cubic yards of concrete, cost $83 million, and employed a peak workforce of about 3,000 (Oregon Encyclopedia). Woody Guthrie was hired by BPA in 1941 to write promotional songs about the dam (Oregon Encyclopedia).
- Displacement: the federal government promised 50 in-lieu fishing sites to Native fishers who lost access to flooded fishing grounds, but only three were initially completed (Oregon Encyclopedia, attributed) — a concrete, sourced replacement for the draft's vague 'forcing the relocation of Native American communities.'
- The Dalles Dam closed its floodgates at 10:00 a.m. on March 10, 1957; the reservoir filled and submerged Celilo Falls (about 40 feet high, horseshoe-shaped) within about 4.5 hours, before at least 10,000 onlookers, along with the villages of Wyam and S'kin (HistoryLink, attributed).
- Compensation for the loss of Celilo Falls fishing grounds totaled a $26.8 million settlement (roughly $3,700 per enrolled member) to the Warm Springs, Yakama, Umatilla, and Nez Perce tribes, who retained their treaty right to fish 'in common' with non-Natives (HistoryLink, attributed).
- CRITFC (the four-tribe fisheries commission of the Yakama, Nez Perce, Warm Springs, and Umatilla) describes Celilo as one of the longest continuously occupied sites on the continent, where as many as 5,000 people gathered seasonally to trade, feast, and hold ceremonies (CRITFC, attributed), with fishing regulated by elders/chiefs and a First Salmon ceremony opening each season.
- Named, dated wind energy projects with federal figures: BPA's Stateline Wind Project (Vansycle Ridge, Walla Walla County WA / Umatilla County OR) — BPA Record of Decision Dec. 13, 2001; BPA purchased power from 137 Vestas V-47 660-kW turbines (90.42 MW) out of a full project of 399 turbines/263 MW; commercial operation began July 2001 (BPA ROD, primary/official).
- DOE's Nov. 9, 2005 Record of Decision approved the Goodnoe Hills (up to 125 MW, 35-50 turbines, near Goldendale) and White Creek (up to 200 MW, 111-133 turbines, near Roosevelt) wind projects in Klickitat County, WA, interconnecting at BPA's new Rock Creek 500/230-kV substation (DOE EIS-0183 ROD, primary/official).
- The federal wind Production Tax Credit was created by the Energy Policy Act of 1992 at $0.015/kWh (inflation-adjusted), available for up to ten years for projects online before July 1, 1999.
