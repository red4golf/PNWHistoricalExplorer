## Facts corrected (found during research)

1. **Salem was designated as the territorial capital in 1850, confirmed permanently by a decisi**
   - Problem: The territorial legislature actually designated Salem on February 1, 1851 (not 1850), and no source confirms a '79%' figure -- the only verifiable detail is that Salem beat the runner-up, Portland, by roughly double the votes in both the 1862 and 1864 elections held under the 1857 state constitution's process.
   - Corrected to: Salem was named territorial capital by legislative act of February 1, 1851; after legal challenges, the 1857 state constitution put the question to voters, and Salem defeated Portland (by roughly double Portland's vote total) in both the 1862 and 1864 elections.
   - Source: Willamette Heritage Center, 'Why is Salem Oregon's Capital?' *(editorial-secondary)*
2. **The first Capitol building, completed in 1854 in Grecian Doric style, burned under suspici**
   - Problem: The building was built as a wood-frame structure (changed from planned ashlar stone to save money), its foundation was laid in 1853, it was only partially occupied by the legislature in December 1854, and it burned December 29, 1855 -- but a formal inquiry at the time found the fire was NOT intentionally set, contradicting 'suspicious circumstances.'
   - Corrected to: The first Capitol, a wood-frame Greek Revival (Doric order) building with its foundation laid in 1853, was partially occupied by the legislature in December 1854 and burned on December 29, 1855, after less than a year of use; arson was suspected at the time but a formal inquiry found the fire was not intentionally set.
   - Source: Willamette Heritage Center, 'State Capitol' *(editorial-secondary)*
3. **The second Capitol, built in Classical Revival style between 1876-1893**
   - Problem: Funds for the second capitol were appropriated in 1872 and construction began in 1873; the building was substantially complete by 1876 (not 'built...between 1876-1893'), and its dome was a later addition completed in 1893. The architects were the Portland firm of Krumbein and Gilbert, not named in the entry.
   - Corrected to: The second Capitol, designed by the Portland firm of Krumbein and Gilbert, was funded starting in 1872, begun in 1873, and substantially complete by 1876; its dome, inspired by the U.S. Capitol, was added in 1893.
   - Source: Willamette Heritage Center, 'State Capitol' *(editorial-secondary)*
4. **The dome itself, rising 166 feet above ground level**
   - Problem: Sources give differing figures for the building's overall height depending on whether the statue is included: Oregon Historical Quarterly (peer-reviewed) gives 168 feet from ground to the top of the statue, with the lantern rising 43 feet above the roofline and the rotunda extending 106 feet to the interior dome top; other secondary sources (SAH Archipedia, Living New Deal) round to 166 feet. The entry states a single figure as settled fact without noting this variance.
   - Corrected to: Sources vary: Oregon Historical Quarterly cites 168 feet from ground level to the top of the Oregon Pioneer statue; other sources round the building's height to 166 feet. Attribute whichever figure is used.
   - Source: Oregon Historical Quarterly 114:1 (Willingham, 2013); Living New Deal *(peer-reviewed)*

## New material found in research

- Architect selection: a nine-person citizens committee ran a nationwide competition in 1935 that drew 123 entries; Francis Keally of New York (with Trowbridge & Livingston) won with a design historians call "stripped Classical" or "Grecian Moderne" (Oregon Historical Quarterly, Willingham, 'Architecture of the Oregon State Capitol,' OHQ 114:1, Spring 2013).
- Construction ran from the contract/groundbreaking of December 4, 1936 to certified completion June 18, 1938 (Willamette Heritage Center; Library of Congress catalog record). The 1935 fire that destroyed the second capitol started in the basement of the east wing on the night of April 25, 1935 (Willamette Heritage Center; Oregon Encyclopedia).
- The second (1876) capitol's architects were the Portland firm of Krumbein and Gilbert; funds were appropriated in 1872, construction began 1873, the building was substantially complete by 1876, and its dome was added in 1893 (Willamette Heritage Center).
- PWA (Public Works Administration/Federal Emergency Administration of Public Works) covered about 45% of the central building's construction cost; that central building cost roughly $2.29-2.5 million (Living New Deal project database, UC Berkeley).
- Oregon Historical Quarterly gives exterior dimensions of 394 feet long by 162 feet wide, a lantern rising 43 feet above the roof, total height ground-to-statue-top of 168 feet, and a rotunda 55 feet in diameter extending 106 feet to the interior dome top; the rotunda dome carries 33 gold-leaf stars representing Oregon as the 33rd state (OHQ, Willingham 2013).
- Rotunda murals (Frank Schwarz and Barry Faulkner, painters) depict: Robert Gray's ship arriving at the Columbia River estuary in 1792 (with the three-masted Columbia Rediviva); Lewis and Clark shown with Sacagawea; Oregon Trail covered wagons; a Senate chamber mural of news of statehood reaching Oregon in 1859; and (per Oregon Encyclopedia) a House chamber mural of the 1843 Champoeg settlers' meeting and 'John McLoughlin Greeting Marcus and Narcissa Whitman.' Sculptors on the building were Ulric Ellerhusen and Leo Friedlander.
- 158 names are inscribed in the friezes above the Senate and House chambers combined (69 in the Senate chamber alone), drawn from Philip Cogswell Jr.'s book 'Capitol Names: Individuals Woven Into Oregon's History' (Oregon Historical Society) -- source: Oregon Legislature, Secretary of the Senate official page.
- The Oregon Pioneer statue: 23 feet 6 inches tall, weighs 17,000 pounds, has an 11-foot axe and 6-foot boots; bronze core, not solid gold. 2025 re-gilding used 23-karat gold leaf; prior re-gildings were 1958, 1984, and 2000; the 2025 project cost $234,375 total ($9,110 for ~11.5 oz of gold leaf), began early July 2025, targeted for completion late August 2025; superintendent Mike Sliwinski noted 'the top of his head has no gold left on it from all of the birds' (Bend Bulletin, July 22, 2025).
- The March 25, 1993 Scotts Mills earthquake (M5.6, 5:34 a.m.) cracked the Capitol dome and shifted the pioneer statue atop it (Pacific Northwest Seismic Network, pnsn.org).
- The current Capitol Accessibility, Maintenance and Safety (CAMS) modernization/seismic project began in 2016, is slated for completion February 2, 2026 (North Plaza reopening mid-March 2026), and cost $598 million total ($465 million Phase 3 approved 2022); work included excavating the basement 20 feet below the original foundation, removing 36,000 cubic yards of soil, and installing 160 base isolators; project director Jodie Jones; new public art dedicated May 26, 2026 through the Percent for Art program includes 'Oregon Voices' (boulders with texts selected by Dr. Phillip Cash Cash, Cayuse/Nez Perce), 'Land, Air, Sea' (bronze paddles by Shirod Younker and Tony A. Johnson), and etched glass by Sara Siestreem, with representatives of seven of Oregon's nine federally recognized tribes at the dedication (constructionowners.com; centraloregondaily.com).
