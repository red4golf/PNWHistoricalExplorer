## Facts corrected

1. **killed Marcus, Narcissa, and eleven others (13 total)**
   - Problem: Death toll varies by source and by what is counted; some deaths occurred over following days, and some scholarly sources give 14.
   - Corrected to: Hedge: 'the Whitmans and about eleven others were killed on and shortly after November 29, 1847 (commonly given as 13, some sources 14).' Do not state a single hard number as settled.
   - Source: NPS (13); HistoryLink File 5192 (14); Oregon Encyclopedia ('eleven or twelve other people') *(editorial-secondary)*
2. **Forty-seven survivors were taken hostage**
   - Problem: Hostage count not uniform (47 to 53).
   - Corrected to: 'roughly 47-53 survivors (about fifty) were taken captive and held about a month before Peter Skene Ogden ransomed them.'
   - Source: Oregon Encyclopedia (53); HistoryLink (~50); NPS (47) *(editorial-secondary)*
3. **guided the first large wagon train of approximately 1,000 settlers west**
   - Problem: The ~1,000 figure is acceptable if softened ('about'), but the surrounding 'Whitman saved Oregon' legend is DISCREDITED; he traveled WITH the 1843 migration as guide/physician, did not single-handedly lead it or 'save' Oregon.
   - Corrected to: 'returned west in 1843 with the Great Migration of roughly 800-1,000 emigrants, serving as guide and physician.' Explicitly avoid/debunk any 'saved Oregon' claim.
   - Source: Oregon Encyclopedia; HistoryLink File 5192; Cassandra Tate, 'Unsettled Ground' *(editorial-secondary)*
4. **The Cayuse War lasted from 1848 to 1850**
   - Problem: Hostilities began December 1847, immediately after the killings.
   - Corrected to: 'Cayuse War, roughly 1847/48-1850.'
   - Source: Oregon Encyclopedia; HistoryLink File 5192 *(editorial-secondary)*
5. **measles arrived at Waiilatpu with a wagon train**
   - Problem: Sources agree measles spread along the emigrant corridor in 1847; framing it as a single wagon-train delivery is not firmly supported (one source says it was introduced by tribesmen, not overlanders).
   - Corrected to: 'measles spread through the region along the emigrant corridor in 1847,' not a single wagon-train delivery event.
   - Source: Oregon Encyclopedia ('Marcus Whitman') *(editorial-secondary)*

## Claims that could not be sourced, and were dropped

- A precise fixed death toll of exactly 13 (or exactly 14) as uncontested.
- The literal, uniform gloss that a Cayuse te-wat was automatically executed for any patient's death (present as a real cultural framework, not a mechanical rule).
- The exact single translation of Waiilatpu ('place of the rye grass' is standard but variants exist).

- The 'Whitman saved Oregon' legend (discredited) - cut entirely.
- Whitman single-handedly guiding or leading the first large wagon train; recast as traveling WITH the 1843 migration as guide and physician.
- A single settled death toll of exactly 13; hedged as commonly 13, some sources 14.
- The 'martyrs on the Oregon Trail' framing (and the replaced martyr-title book).
- 'Measles arrived at Waiilatpu with a wagon train' as a single delivery event; recast as regional corridor spread in 1847.
- 'Whitman Massacre' as the sole/settled name; presented as long-used but now contested.
- Evaluative framing words: 'tragic,' 'heroic,' 'savage,' 'treacherous,' 'martyr.'

## New material found in research

- The te-wat/medicine-man framework as the interpretive hinge (NPS Cayuse Teacher Guide), with the scholarly caution (OE) that it was not an automatic rule - two worldviews colliding, not good vs. evil.
- The epidemic asymmetry (about half the nearby Cayuse dead vs. nearly all emigrants recovering) - one well-sourced statistic that makes the Cayuse response comprehensible.
- The shifting legacy / naming story: NPS reinterpreted the site, OE now says 'Whitman Murders' - exactly Cassandra Tate's subject.
- The Cayuse Five, named, with contested-guilt framing and the recent CTUIR memorial in Oregon City - a living-tribe voice closing the loop from 1847 to today.
- The 'first white American women across the Continental Divide' detail (South Pass, 1836) - notable and verifiable, worded plainly.
