## Facts corrected (found during research)

1. **Italian-born artist Attilio Pusterla painted the detailed spiral frieze**
   - Problem: Pusterla did not paint the frieze; he executed it in sgraffito, a technique of cutting through a wet plaster layer to expose a dark base coat.
   - Corrected to: Pusterla created the frieze using sgraffito (plaster-cutting), described by the Oregon Encyclopedia as 'the world's only large-scale pictorial frieze in sgraffito.'
   - Source: Oregon Encyclopedia, 'Astoria Column' *(editorial-secondary)*
2. **the Astoria Column rises 125 feet above the Columbia River**
   - Problem: 125 feet is the column's own height, not its elevation above the river; the entry conflates the two, and the column sits atop Coxcomb Hill, not at river level.
   - Corrected to: The column itself stands 125 feet tall, atop Coxcomb Hill overlooking the Columbia River.
   - Source: Oregon Encyclopedia, 'Astoria Column'; NRHP nomination, ref. 74001681 *(editorial-secondary)*
3. **The War of 1812 disrupted Astor's plans when British forces captured Fort Astoria**
   - Problem: Fort Astoria was not taken by British military action. The North West Company purchased it from the financially strained Pacific Fur Company about two months before a British warship arrived; the British captain's later flag-raising was a symbolic act over an already-purchased post, not a capture.
   - Corrected to: The North West Company, a British firm, bought Fort Astoria from the Pacific Fur Company in 1813 shortly before a British warship arrived; British Capt. William Black formally (and largely symbolically) took possession on December 12, 1813, renaming it Fort George. The U.S. flag was restored in October 1818.
   - Source: Oregon Encyclopedia, 'Fort George (Fort Astoria)' *(editorial-secondary)*
4. **climb its 164 steps**
   - Problem: Sources disagree: the Oregon Encyclopedia (as currently maintained) gives 164 interior steps, but the 1974 National Register nomination describes '166 steps in steel circular stairway.' The staircase was reportedly replaced in 2008, which may account for the discrepancy.
   - Corrected to: Report the step count as approximately 164-166, noting the discrepancy between the current Oregon Encyclopedia figure (164) and the 1974 NRHP nomination figure (166), possibly reflecting the 2008 staircase replacement.
   - Source: Oregon Encyclopedia, 'Astoria Column'; NRHP nomination, ref. 74001681 *(editorial-secondary)*

## New material found in research

- NRHP nomination (1974, ref. 74001681): column base is 19'6" square and 13'6" high, the tapering barrel runs 94'6" from 13'6" diameter at bottom to 11'4" at top, and the spiral frieze itself is 7 feet wide and 525 feet long — a level of physical specificity the current entry lacks entirely.
- Exact funding breakdown (Oregon Encyclopedia): total construction cost was $27,133.96; Vincent Astor gave $20,000; architect Electus D. Litchfield contributed $200 of his own fee; the Great Northern Railway covered the remaining balance and managed the project. Proposed in 1925 by Great Northern Railway president Ralph Budd.
- Dedication: July 22, 1926, during the three-day Astoria Founders Celebration (Oregon Encyclopedia).
- Pusterla did not paint the frieze — he executed it in sgraffito, cutting outlines through a wet plaster top layer to expose a dark base coat beneath; Oregon Encyclopedia calls it 'the world's only large-scale pictorial frieze in sgraffito.'
- The NRHP nomination lists the frieze's ten discrete narrative scenes bottom-to-top: primeval forest; Robert Gray's discovery (1792); Lewis and Clark Expedition (1805); construction of Fort Clatsop (1805-06); Astor's ship Tonquin (1810); founding of Fort Astoria (1811); the Pacific Fur Company's overland party arrival (1812); the Tonquin's explosion (1811); restoration of Fort Astoria to the U.S. (1818); and the coming of settlers.
- The column was modeled on Trajan's Column in Rome (erected under Emperor Trajan, NRHP nomination gives circa 114 A.D.); Astoria historian John Goodenberger has drawn the contrast that 'while the Trajan column was about war, the Astoria column was about peace' (The Astorian, May 21, 2026) — worth using as an attributed quote rather than an unsourced claim.
- By 1936 the sgraffito had deteriorated enough to require repair work; a major 1995 restoration led by Frank Preusser of the J. Paul Getty Museum cost over $1 million and found only about 20 percent of the original sgraffito surviving; the restored column was unveiled November 19, 1995. A further restoration focused on the sgraffito took place in 2015.
- Fort Astoria was not captured by British military force in the War of 1812: the North West Company purchased the post from the financially exposed Pacific Fur Company roughly two months before a British warship (HMS Racoon, under Capt. William Black) arrived on November 30, 1813; Black took formal, largely symbolic possession on December 12, 1813, renaming it Fort George. The U.S. flag was restored in October 1818 when agent John B. Prevost arrived (Oregon Encyclopedia, 'Fort George'). This corrects the entry's 'British forces captured Fort Astoria.'
- Fort Clatsop, the expedition's winter quarters, was a 50-foot-square log structure; the party of 32 men, one woman (Sacagawea), her infant, and a dog moved in on Christmas Day, December 25, 1805, and departed March 23, 1806; rain fell on all but about 12 days of their roughly four-month stay (NPS, Lewis and Clark National Historical Park).
- Columbia River salmon canning: 8 canneries operated by 1873, rising to 39 by 1883; in 1883 alone the industry produced 629,400 cases of canned Chinook salmon worth $3,147,000 (Oregon Encyclopedia, commercial fishing history).
