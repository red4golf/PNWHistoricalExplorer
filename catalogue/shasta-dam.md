## New material found in research

- Frank Crowe, the construction director/superintendent overseeing Shasta Dam's overall construction (previously known for work on Hoover Dam-era projects for Six Companies/Morrison-Knudsen), directed installation of the 460-foot cableway head tower system; he retired after completing Shasta in 1944 and died in February 1946. (ASCE professional-society bio; corroborate specific role/dates against Stene 1996 before using verbatim -- treat ASCE as editorial-secondary and attribute.)
- Concrete placement milestones with exact dates: first concrete July 8, 1940; one millionth cubic yard May 3, 1941; two millionth cubic yard October 13, 1941; three millionth cubic yard March 28, 1942; four millionth cubic yard August 31, 1942; six millionth cubic yard December 23, 1943; final concrete on the dam structure itself December 22, 1944 (Vista House concrete finished January 1945; Reclamation formally accepted the dam and powerplant concrete work from Pacific Constructors June 20, 1945). Source: Stene 1996, Bureau of Reclamation.
- During WWII, two generators and a turbine slated for Shasta Powerplant were instead diverted to Grand Coulee Dam in Washington because Grand Coulee could start generating power sooner (spring 1943) than Shasta (not until 1944) -- a concrete, dated irony that undercuts a simple 'wartime urgency accelerated Shasta' narrative. Source: Stene 1996.
- The government construction camps and boomtowns: Project City developed by William and Gene Hammans; Summit City at the intersection of Shasta Dam Blvd. and Kennett-Buckeye Road; the community called 'Central Valley' (originally nicknamed 'Boomtown'); named saloons/entertainment spots Charley's Place, The Mint Pool Hall, The Round-Up; the 'Hell's Gulch' festival (1938-1941) in which a coffin representing 'old man gloom' was ceremonially burned; building lots advertised at $95 with $5 down and $5/month; Toyon School principal Matt Rumboltz recalling 'some classes had over 70 students!' Source: Al Rocca, City of Shasta Lake official history.
- Reclamation Commissioner John C. Page formally named the dam 'Shasta' (after Mount Shasta) at a September 12, 1937 ceremony; the unofficial working name 'Kennett' derived from a Southern Pacific Railroad way station in Sacramento Canyon just above the dam site, and the original site studied was called the Kennett site, requiring relocation of about 30 miles of the Southern Pacific's line. Source: Stene 1996.
- 308 human remains (173 Native American, 135 white) were exhumed and relocated from the area to be flooded by Shasta Lake into two newly established cemeteries -- Central Valley Cemetery and the United States Shasta Reservoir Indian Cemetery -- a specific, dated, and named consequence of the reservoir's creation, independently corroborated by both a 1996 Reclamation project history and a 2025 Reclamation newsroom release. Source: Stene 1996; Bureau of Reclamation newsroom release 5187.
- Winnemem Wintu spiritual leader Caleen Sisk-Franco and Tribal Headman Mark Franco were interviewed by geographer Bradley L. Garrett for his 2009/2010 peer-reviewed study on the tribe's submerged ancestral places along the McCloud River, flooded 'just after World War II' by Shasta Dam; useful as an attributed anchor for the indigenous-impact section rather than the current entry's unsourced generalities. Source: Garrett, Archaeologies 6(2), 2010.
- Physical dimensions for the dam (verify each figure against at least two graded sources before publishing, since sources show some internal inconsistency -- see corrected/unverifiable notes): height 602 feet; crest/dam length approximately 3,460 feet (per Bureau of Reclamation newsroom release and ASCE bio of Frank Crowe -- note NPS's page separately and inconsistently lists 'Length: 487 feet,' which appears to duplicate its own 'Spillway length: 487 feet' figure and should not be used for total dam length); base thickness 883 feet; crest thickness 30 feet; total concrete volume 6.5 million cubic yards (about 15 million tons), per NPS.
- Shasta Powerplant's original capacity was 539,000 kilowatts per the 1996 Reclamation history (Stene), while a 2025 Reclamation newsroom release lists 710 megawatts and states the facility is the largest federally owned hydroelectric facility in California, powering between 355,000 and 710,000 homes and generating $50-100 million in annual federal revenue -- these are different eras/uprated figures and should be dated and attributed rather than merged into one undated number.
