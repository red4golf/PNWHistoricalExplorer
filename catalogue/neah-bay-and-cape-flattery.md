## Facts corrected (found during research)

1. **Standing at Cape Flattery... you're on land that has been home to the Makah people for ove**
   - Problem: No graded source supports a flat 'over 2,000 years' figure for the whole of Makah presence generally; the best-documented figure is for the Ozette site specifically.
   - Corrected to: EBSCO Research Starters (drawing on the Ozette excavation) documents occupation at the Ozette village site from the first centuries C.E. through the 1920s -- a span of roughly 1,800-2,000 years. Attribute this specifically to Ozette, not as a blanket figure for 'the Makah people' as a whole.
   - Source: EBSCO Research Starters, 'Ozette (archaeological site)', 2023 *(editorial-secondary)*
2. **The Makah people have inhabited the rugged coastline around Neah Bay since time immemorial**
   - Problem: 'Since time immemorial' is unattributed legend/booster language, not a sourced claim.
   - Corrected to: Drop the phrase, or attribute explicitly if quoting a Makah tribal source that uses it.
   - Source: N/A *(editorial-secondary)*
3. **Spanish explorer Juan Pérez first documented contact with the Makah in 1774, followed by C**
   - Problem: Neither claim is supported. HistoryLink's account of Perez's 1774 voyage documents him sighting the Strait of Juan de Fuca and coastal territory; his only documented Native contact was with the Haida at Nootka Sound -- no Makah contact described. Cook named Cape Flattery on March 22, 1778 but explicitly did not land or make contact with the Makah; he turned away after judging there was no harbor.
   - Corrected to: Perez (1774) sighted the coast and the Strait of Juan de Fuca; sources found document no direct contact with the Makah. Cook (March 22, 1778) named Cape Flattery from offshore but did not land or make contact with the Makah -- correct 'contact' to 'sighted'/'named' and drop or heavily qualify any claim of a documented meeting.
   - Source: HistoryLink.org File 5677 (Perez); HistoryLink.org File 5035 (Cook) *(editorial-secondary)*
4. **The lighthouse's operation required Makah cooperation... Makah pilots, drawing on generati**
   - Problem: No source found supports a cooperative-partnership framing or a claim of Makah pilots guiding ships. The detailed construction source instead documents conflict: builders armed themselves with muskets and built a blockhouse against Tatoosh Island's Makah residents, with relations reportedly not improving until 1885.
   - Corrected to: Construction (1856-57) involved documented conflict with Makah residents of Tatoosh Island, not straightforward cooperation. No source found substantiates Makah tribal members serving as ship pilots.
   - Source: HistoryLink.org File 5703 *(editorial-secondary)*
5. **The Cape Flattery Trail, managed jointly by the Makah Tribe and Olympic National Park**
   - Problem: Incorrect. The trail is located entirely on Makah Reservation land, outside Olympic National Park boundaries, and is managed solely by the Makah Tribe (parking permits issued by the Tribe).
   - Corrected to: The Cape Flattery Trail is on Makah Reservation land, outside Olympic National Park, managed by the Makah Tribe. NPS lists it as a nearby point of interest but does not manage it.
   - Source: NPS, 'Cape Flattery Trailhead' *(primary-official)*
6. **The Makah are the only Native American tribe with treaty-protected whaling rights**
   - Problem: Overbroad superlative; the sourced comparison found is regional (Washington coast), not national.
   - Corrected to: Reframe as: described as the only Indigenous group on the Washington coast whose treaty explicitly named the right to hunt whales (attributed).
   - Source: KNKX Public Radio (2023) *(editorial-secondary)*
7. **A mudslide had preserved Makah artifacts for 500 years in pristine condition [at Ozette, d**
   - Problem: The '500 years' figure is corroborated by the Makah Museum's radiocarbon estimate (slide dated 500+/-50 years before present) and broadly consistent with EBSCO's dating of the slide to the 15th century C.E. One secondary source (Washington State Magazine, 2009) instead dates the slide to January 26, 1700 -- only ~270 years before the 1970 discovery, contradicting the 500-year figure. Sources conflict.
   - Corrected to: Keep the general '~500 years' framing (tribal institution + academic secondary source) but do not assign a specific calendar date, which conflicts across sources.
   - Source: Makah Museum (500+/-50 BP); EBSCO (15th century); cf. Washington State Magazine (2009, gives 1700 -- conflicting) *(editorial-secondary)*
8. **a 27,000-acre reservation that included their traditional whaling grounds [established by **
   - Problem: Per HistoryLink, the reservation as initially defined in 1855 was a small tract near Cape Flattery (excluding traditional villages except Neah); ~27,000 acres reflects a later enlargement, not the original 1855 grant.

## New material found in research

- Named 1999 harpooner Theron Parker and crewmember Donnie Swan; the canoe was named Hummingbird; the whale was struck off Cape Alava on the morning of May 17, 1999, before protest boats arrived, and towed to Neah Bay (HistoryLink File 5310).
- Precise Ozette excavation facts: directed by WSU archaeologist Richard 'Doc' Daugherty (with geologist Roald Fryxell), 11-year dig (1970-1981), roughly 40,000-55,000 artifacts recovered, all retained on the Makah reservation (Makah Museum; Washington State Magazine).
- Exact treaty language: Article 4 secures 'the right of taking fish and of whaling or sealing at usual and accustomed grounds and stations,' 'in common with all citizens' -- quotable primary language rather than paraphrase.
- Cape Flattery Light specifics: first-order Fresnel lens made in Paris in 1854 by Louis Sautter & Company; light first shown December 28, 1857; steam foghorn installed 1872 for $10,000; oscillating light replaced fixed light in 1914.
- Documented conflict during lighthouse construction: builders armed with muskets, built a blockhouse against Tatoosh Island's Makah residents, relations reportedly not improving until 1885 -- a specific, non-romanticized episode.
- 1997 U.S.-Russia agreement gave the Makah a quota of four gray whales annually out of a shared North Pacific indigenous allotment of 124 whales under the IWC.
- Cook named Cape Flattery on March 22, 1778 specifically because a promising 'opening' turned out to have no harbor -- his own recorded reasoning, and he did not land.
- Traditional whaling gear specifics from the Makah Tribe's own account: harpoons 12-18 feet long, yew wood shafts, elk-antler barbs, mussel-shell components, sealskin floats, sinew rope up to 40 fathoms bound with cherry bark, eight-person canoe crews.
- 1999 hunt opposition scale: over 350 organizations from 27 countries opposed resumption; a candlelight vigil was held in Seattle.
- Cape Flattery Trail is precisely 3/4 mile (1.2 km) each way, on Makah Reservation land, requiring a Tribe-issued parking permit -- concrete visitor-facing facts from NPS's own trailhead page.
