## New material found in research

- Sawtooth National Recreation Area established by Public Law 92-400, enacted August 22, 1972 — the act creates the NRA by reference to a June 1972 map rather than a fixed acreage figure, with the Sawtooth Wilderness (superseding the 1937 Sawtooth Primitive Area) designated within it. (govinfo.gov, 86 Stat. 612)
- Legislative history with named people: Senator Frank Church (D-ID) was the primary champion, first proposing national-park legislation in 1960 before shifting to a recreation-area strategy; co-sponsors included Senator Len B. Jordan, Rep. James McClure, and Rep. Orval Hansen; Idaho Governor Cecil Andrus opposed mining development and backed protection. A 1968 ASARCO proposal for an open-pit molybdenum mine in the White Clouds is credited with galvanizing bipartisan support for the Act. (Boise State University Andrus Center whitepaper — must attribute in text)
- Sawtooth batholith geology: USGS geologists describe the pink granite's age as 'not known with certainty but considered to be Tertiary'; one geologist proposed an age of about 59 million years. The pink color comes from perthitic orthoclase; it's a 'tin granite' with anomalous beryllium, molybdenum, uranium and niobium, though no economic deposits were found. (USGS Bulletin 1319-D)
- Town of Stanley/Stanley Basin origin: gold prospects first found in Stanley Basin in 1863 when John Stanley led a prospecting party there (town named for him); party didn't return to develop claims until 1864. Hydraulic 'giant' mining at Robinson Bar was running by 1868; by 1871 claims yielded '$20 per day to the hand.' Dredge operations at Stanley began September 24, 1899. (Idaho State Historical Society Reference Series #365)
- Sawtooth City mining camp: explored by Levi Smiley spring 1878; rush began spring 1879; formally organized at a miners' meeting November 29, 1879. By August 1881 it had 25 houses, a meat market, assay office, blacksmith shop, Chinese laundry, general store, two restaurants and three saloons; peak population ~600. A ten-stamp mill cost $50,000. Deserted by 1888 except the Silver King mine, ended by a disastrous 1892 fire. Vienna (8 miles away): a twenty-stamp mill produced $40,000-50,000/month in 1883, total production over $500,000 in silver, shut down May 1885 amid litigation. (ISHS Reference Series #207 / NRHP nomination for Sawtooth City)
- Custer/Bonanza/Yankee Fork district: General Custer mine discovered August 17, 1876 by James Baxter, E.M. Dodge, and Morgan McKeim; Bonanza City grew up in summer 1877 near the Charles Dickens vein (found 1875 by W.A. Norton). By July 1879 combined population reached about 2,000. Custer mine's lifetime production totaled around $8 million. The Dickens-Custer Company shut down October 1892; Custer and Bonanza became ghost towns after 1912. (ISHS Reference Series #204)
- Yankee Fork Gold Dredge: a 988-ton dredge that (per Idaho Dept. of Parks and Recreation's official page) operated through 1952, mining 6,330,000 cubic yards of stream gravel and leaving 5 miles of tailings; recovered an estimated $1,037,322 in gold and silver against an operating cost of $1,076,100. Sunbeam Dam, built 1910 to power nearby mines, was breached in 1934 and is described as 'the only dam ever constructed on the Salmon [River].' (Note: a Friends-of-Idaho-State-Parks PDF gives conflicting dredge dimensions/years — use only the state agency's official page as authoritative.)
- Sockeye salmon and 'Lonesome Larry': Idaho Fish and Game states an estimated 25,000-35,000 sockeye returned annually to the Sawtooth Valley/Payette Basin in the 1880s, before dams. Species listed endangered in 1991. In 1992, only one sockeye — nicknamed 'Lonesome Larry' — returned to Redfish Lake. Sockeye migrate more than 900 miles from the ocean and climb more than 6,500 feet in elevation. Recovery captured 16 remaining adults 1991-98; by 2014, 460 naturally spawned sockeye returned (a program record), with an overall record return of about 1,600 fish that year. (Idaho Fish and Game; NOAA Fisheries)
- Sawtooth Wilderness: 217,000 acres, established by the 1972 Act, superseding a 'Primitive Area' first protected in 1937. Contains 50 peaks over 10,000 feet and nearly 400 high alpine lakes (a subset of the ~300+ across the whole NRA), with nearly 350 miles of trail. (fs.usda.gov/r04/sawtooth/recreation/sawtooth-wilderness)
- Tribal association and treaty rights: a Boise State University Andrus Center whitepaper states Shoshone-Bannock ancestors provided stewardship of the Sawtooth region for at least 10,000 years before Euro-American arrival, harvesting camas bulbs and Chinook/sockeye salmon; after forced relocation to the Fort Hall Reservation, the Fort Bridger Treaty of 1868 preserved rights to return for subsistence harvesting. Must be attributed in text as an academic/editorial secondary source (not independently confirmed against the Tribes' own site, which could not be fetched this session).
