## Facts corrected (found during research)

1. **Fort Worden commanded the north arm of the Triangle of Fire from 1897 to 1953**
   - Problem: 1897 was when site work/construction was authorized/begun, not when Fort Worden was operational. The fort was not activated as a garrisoned post until May 14, 1902; active military operation ran 1902-1953, not 1897-1953.
   - Corrected to: Congress authorized the three-fort Puget Sound defense system in 1896; construction at Fort Worden began in 1897, but the post was not activated/garrisoned until May 14, 1902, and operated as an active defense installation until its June 30, 1953 closure.
   - Source: Washington State Parks, Fort Worden Historical State Park History; HistoryLink.org File 7524 *(primary-official)*
2. **constructed between 1908-1910 at a cost of $207,832.50**
   - Problem: No graded source (NRHP nomination, HistoryLink, CDSG, USACE) supports the specific $207,832.50 figure or a 1908-1910 construction span; the 1976 NRHP nomination gives 1908 as the completion year for Battery Kinzie with no construction-cost figure provided. This dollar figure could not be independently verified anywhere and appears only in the current draft and its own source app.
   - Corrected to: Battery Kinzie was completed in 1908 (drop the cost figure and the 1908-1910 range as unverifiable).
   - Source: National Register of Historic Places Inventory-Nomination Form, Fort Worden Historic District (1976) *(primary-official)*
3. **Named for Brigadier General David Hunter Kinzie, a Civil War veteran who died in 1904**
   - Problem: The death year (1904) is plausible and appears consistently in specialist military-history sources (Coast Defense Study Group; Antietam on the Web), but neither of those sites meets the assignment's graded-source tiers (not primary/official, not peer-reviewed, not HistoryLink/Oregon Encyclopedia-equivalent editorial-secondary). No NPS, NRHP, or HistoryLink document independently confirms Kinzie's biography. Treat as unverifiable per the strict standard rather than carried forward as fact.
   - Corrected to: Battery Kinzie is named for Brig. Gen. David H. Kinzie, U.S. Army (namesake confirmed by battery naming convention at Fort Worden per NRHP nomination, which names batteries after Army officers); specific biographical details (Civil War service, birth/death dates) could not be confirmed against a graded source and should be dropped or heavily hedged.
   - Source: National Register of Historic Places Inventory-Nomination Form, Fort Worden Historic District (1976) confirms only that batteries are named for officers; biographical specifics unconfirmed *(primary-official)*
4. **Battery Kinzie achieved unexpected fame in 1982 when it served as the dramatic backdrop fo**
   - Problem: HistoryLink's detailed account of the film's Port Townsend production (filming began late April 1981, released 1982) lists Fort Worden's parade ground and historic buildings, the Port Townsend Paper Mill, Town Tavern, and Tides Inn as filming locations, but does not mention Battery Kinzie specifically, and does not place the 'I got nowhere else to go' scene there. That scene is associated with the paper-mill location in available filming accounts. This specific pairing of Battery Kinzie with that scene is unconfirmed and should be dropped.
   - Corrected to: Fort Worden (its parade ground and historic buildings) was used as a filming location for An Officer and a Gentleman, which began filming in Port Townsend in late April 1981 and was released in 1982 -- but do not attribute the specific 'I got nowhere else to go' scene to Battery Kinzie without further confirmation.
   - Source: HistoryLink.org File 8237 *(editorial-secondary)*
5. **The fort's 41 artillery pieces were distributed among 12 batteries, including Batteries As**
   - Problem: Spelling error: the battery is 'Brannan,' not 'Brannon,' per both the 1976 NRHP nomination and the Puget Sound Coast Artillery Museum's own visitor materials.
   - Corrected to: ...including Batteries Ash, Powell, Brannan, Quarles, Randol, Vicars, Tolles, Stoddard, Benson, Putnam, Walker, and Kinzie (12 total).
   - Source: National Register of Historic Places Inventory-Nomination Form, Fort Worden Historic District (1976) *(primary-official)*
6. **The guns fell silent in 1944 when changing military technology made fixed coastal defenses**
   - Problem: Not confirmed by any graded source consulted. HistoryLink and the NRHP nomination both point to the fort's formal deactivation as June 30, 1953, with no independent confirmation of a distinct 1944 date for guns 'falling silent.' This specific date/claim could not be verified and should be dropped or replaced with the confirmed 1953 closure.
   - Corrected to: Fort Worden was deactivated as an active military post on June 30, 1953.
   - Source: HistoryLink.org File 7524 *(editorial-secondary)*
7. **The lighthouse keeper also served as an unofficial harbor pilot, providing guidance to ves**
   - Problem: HistoryLink's detailed Point Wilson Lighthouse essay contains no reference to a keeper acting as an unofficial harbor pilot, nor to keeper-family integration with Fort Worden social/school life. These claims appear to be unsupported embellishment with no traceable source and should be dropped entirely.
   - Corrected to: Drop these claims; no graded source supports them.
   - Source: HistoryLink.org File 5680 (absence of any such claim after full-text review) *(editorial-secondary)*
8. **The lighthouse was equipped with a fourth-order Fresnel lens... manufactured in Paris by B**
   - Problem: The fourth-order Fresnel lens itself is confirmed, but the specific manufacturer attribution (Barbier, Benard & Turenne, Paris) does not appear in HistoryLink's account or the U.S. Coast Guard's asset page and could not be independently verified against a graded source in this research pass.
   - Corrected to: Fourth-order Fresnel lens (confirmed); manufacturer attribution should be dropped unless confirmed elsewhere.
   - Source: HistoryLink.org File 5680; USCG Historian's Office Point Wilson Lighthouse page *(editorial-secondary)*
9. **The automation of Point Wilson Lighthouse in 1976 ended over six decades of resident light**
   - Problem: Sources disagree on the exact automation year: HistoryLink's detailed essay states automation occurred in November 1976, while the U.S. Coast Guard's own historian's-office asset page states 1977. This is a real discrepancy between two editorial/official sources rather than a simple confirmation, and should be flagged rather than stated as settled fact.
   - Corrected to: Point Wilson Lighthouse was automated in either November 1976 (per HistoryLink) or 1977 (per U.S. Coast Guard); attribute whichever figure is used, or note the discrepancy.
   - Source: HistoryLink.org File 5680 vs. U.S. Coast Guard Historian's Office asset page (1976601) *(editorial-secondary)*

## New material found in research

- The SS Governor–SS West Hartland collision, April 1, 1921, 12:04 a.m.: keeper William J. Thomas heard the collision and telephoned Port Townsend authorities; the 417-foot Governor, carrying 240 people, sank within 30 minutes in 240 feet of water with 8 lives lost. (HistoryLink.org, attributed)
- First Point Wilson keeper David M. Littlefield (1840-1913), Civil War veteran, appointed 1879 at $800/year, served 4 years; later Jefferson County Sheriff, Port Townsend mayor, city councilman, and Collector of Customs; married Maria C. Hastings, daughter of Port Townsend founder Loren B. Hastings. (HistoryLink.org, attributed)
- Fort Worden's land was set aside far earlier than usually assumed: President Andrew Johnson's September 22, 1866 executive order withdrew the site (with 23 others around Puget Sound) as a military reservation, decades before any fort was built. (Washington State Parks, official)
- Fort Worden's first garrison: the 126th Coast Artillery Company, 87 soldiers under Capt. Manus McCloskey, arrived May 3, 1902, days before the post's May 14, 1902 activation. (HistoryLink.org, attributed / Washington State Parks, official)
- Battery Kinzie was the last Endicott-period battery built in Washington State and, at roughly 350 feet long and 25 feet high, the largest single battery at Fort Worden and the largest coastal battery in Puget Sound. (1976 National Register of Historic Places nomination, primary/official)
- Full named list and specs of all 12 Fort Worden batteries with individual construction start dates and dimensions (Ash, Benson, Brannan, Kinzie, Powell, Putnam, Quarles, Randol, Stoddard, Tolles, Vicars, Walker) is available from the 1976 NRHP nomination — a rich, precise alternative to the vague 41-guns/12-batteries line in the current draft.
- During WWII the Point Wilson light itself was blacked out as a defensive measure protecting Fort Worden and the wider Puget Sound. (HistoryLink.org, attributed)
- An Officer and a Gentleman began filming in Port Townsend in late April 1981 (released 1982); Fort Worden's parade ground and historic buildings were used, along with the Port Townsend Paper Mill, Town Tavern, and Tides Inn; production brought roughly $3 million to the local economy over a two-month shoot. (HistoryLink.org, attributed)
- 1894: the lighthouse tender Manzanita installed a new revolving fourth-order Fresnel lens at Point Wilson, replacing the fixed 1879 lens. (HistoryLink.org, attributed)
- Point Wilson Light Station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1971 (NRHP #71000870) and the Washington Heritage Register on March 24, 1971. (HistoryLink.org / state register, attributed)
