## Facts corrected

1. **The most profound story of Multnomah Falls comes from the Multnomah people ... [the maiden**
   - Problem: Authoritative sources do not treat this as documented Chinookan oral tradition. The popular written version descends from Victorian 'Indian romance' literature (e.g., Frederic Homer Balch's 1890 'The Bridge of the Gods'); versions vary and are attributed variously to Multnomah, Wasco, and Klickitat peoples. The Oregon Encyclopedia warns Balch's work 'is not to be taken as reliable history.'
   - Corrected to: Present it as a popularly-told legend of uncertain, likely romanticized provenance - widely republished in tourist materials, versions differing across sources - NOT verified Chinookan canon. Strip the sacred/romantic editorializing and the unverifiable specifics.
   - Source: The Oregon Encyclopedia ('Multnomah' and 'Frederic Homer Balch') *(editorial-secondary)*
2. **The basalt cliffs that frame the falls were formed by massive lava flows 17-12 million yea**
   - Problem: Imprecise. The specific flows exposed at Multnomah Falls are the Grande Ronde Basalt (~16.7-15.6 Ma); the CRBG as a whole spans ~16.7-5.5 Ma, with the bulk in a ~1.1-million-year burst.
   - Corrected to: Six Grande Ronde Basalt flows, part of the Columbia River Basalt Group, erupted ~16.7-15.6 million years ago (bulk), later shaped by the Missoula floods.
   - Source: USGS Cascades Volcano Observatory; Kasbohm & Schoene, Science Advances 2018 *(peer-reviewed)*

## New material found in research

- The sourced height with the tier math: 620 ft = 542 ft upper + 9 ft middle cascade + 69 ft lower.
- The hydrology hook: year-round underground springs from Larch Mountain plus rain and snowmelt, so flow peaks in winter and spring.
- Larch Mountain naming irony: a 4,061-ft peak named for larch that don't grow there, because loggers sold noble fir as 'larch.'
- Two-layer geology: six Grande Ronde Basalt flows (~16.7-15.6 Ma) later scoured and steepened by the Ice Age Missoula floods ~15,000 years ago.
- The Lodge: a 1925 lodge by noted Portland architect A.E. Doyle, on the National Register.
- The honest handling of the legend as a case study in how tourist-era romanticism gets mistaken for Native tradition - a more defensible angle than retelling it as fact.
- Deep human presence: the Gorge has drawn people for 13,000+ years; the Multnomah were a Chinookan people ('those toward water').
