## Facts corrected

1. **a 511-foot steel arch over Canoe Pass**
   - Problem: Length wrong. The Canoe Pass structure IS a steel arch, but the arch main span is 350 feet per the primary HABS/HAER record (plus concrete approach spans). '511' is a mis-propagated tourism-source number.
   - Corrected to: 350-foot steel two-hinged deck arch over Canoe Pass (plus concrete approach spans).
   - Source: HABS/HAER No. WA-101 *(primary-official)*
2. **a 976-foot cantilever truss over Deception Pass**
   - Problem: Not supported by the primary record. The Deception Pass structure IS a steel cantilever truss, but its main cantilever unit is 550 feet (two 175-ft arms + a 200-ft suspended center span), ~900 ft of steel with approach trusses. '975/976' appears only in secondary tourism sources for the whole Pass-Island-to-Whidbey length.
   - Corrected to: Steel cantilever truss with a 550-foot main span unit (a 200-ft suspended span between two 175-ft cantilever arms); ~900 ft of steel including approach spans.
   - Source: HABS/HAER No. WA-101 *(primary-official)*
3. **with funding from the federal Public Works Administration**
   - Problem: Imprecise as an exclusive claim. PWA was one part of a New Deal funding mix: HABS records a PWA reimbursement of ~$87,000 against a bridge contract of $304,755 (total project ~$482,000, also state highway funds).
   - Corrected to: Financed by New Deal federal and state funds, including a PWA reimbursement (~$87,000); the bridge contract was $304,755.
   - Source: HABS/HAER No. WA-101; HistoryLink.org Essay 5698 *(primary-official)*

## New material found in research

- The naming irony: the pass literally 'deceived' Vancouver's party into mapping Whidbey as a peninsula until Joseph Whidbey rowed through - the name is a confession of a mapping error.
- A clean 'who built what' that corrects a common myth: PWA/New Deal money + the Puget Construction Company built the bridge in under a year (Aug 1934-July 1935); the CCC built the park's cabins, trails, restrooms, and approach roads - not the span.
- The engineering: two dissimilar structures meeting at rocky Pass Island - a 350-ft steel arch over Canoe Pass and a 550-ft cantilever (a 200-ft suspended span slung between two 175-ft arms) over Deception Pass, ~180 ft above the tidal current.
- Designer worth naming: O. R. Elwell, bridge engineer, Washington Department of Highways.
- Coast Salish waters: the Samish and Swinomish and their neighbors navigated these currents long before 1792.
