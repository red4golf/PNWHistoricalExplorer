The standard

"The witness with a notebook" — derived from Port Madison and Bowerman

Not invented. Every rule below is a description of something your two hand-written entries actually do, measured against the two worst-performing entries for contrast.

The opening move

Three sentences, roughly 20 / 10 / 40 words, in that order. First: place the reader physically — bay, bluff, bearing, distance, airport identifier, town — in present tense, with no adjective and no participle. Second: a short flat sentence about what it looks like now, ideally one that lowers expectation ('It looks like a place where nothing much ever happened') or sets up a reversal. Third: turn on 'In fact' or 'But', and pay out the one thing this entry exists to tell, carrying two or three specific nouns, dates, or names in a single run. Do not state the theme, do not use the word 'significance', and do not name the category the place belongs to. Port Madison and Bowerman both do exactly this; Bowerman's turn lands one paragraph later ('But for decades, Bowerman's true claim to fame wasn't its runway specs or instrument approaches. It was Lana's Cafe.'), which is the acceptable variant.

1. Open with something you could stand next to: a name, a compass bearing, a distance, a date. Present tense, no adjective, no participle.

Both exemplars locate the reader physically in sentence one before asking for any attention. It also gives the narrator a clean first breath in audio.

'On a narrow arm of Port Madison Bay, at the north end of Bainbridge Island, sits a quiet neighborhood of waterfront homes.' / 'Bowerman Airport (KHQM) sits just two miles west of Hoquiam, Washington.'

2. Vary sentence length violently. After any sentence over 30 words, put one under 10.

Sentence-length standard deviation is 9.7 (Port Madison) and 10.2 (Bowerman) against 6.7 and 5.9 in the weak pair. About a third of exemplar sentences are 12 words or shorter; the weak entries contain zero sentences under 13 words. That flatness is what makes them unreadable aloud — there is nowhere to breathe.

'Meigs built fast. By 1858 the mill was cutting 15,000 board feet a day and ranked among the leading plants on the Sound.'

3. Give every action a named human subject and a plain past-tense verb. If you cannot name who did it, you probably cannot source it either.

Named actors make claims falsifiable, which is the accuracy requirement, and they make sentences move, which is the readability requirement. Same move solves both.

'In 1854 he bought J. J. Felt's sawmill, moved it to Port Madison, and never really left.' Not: 'Community volunteers contributed thousands of hours to restoration efforts.'

4. Carry emotion with an itemized list of physical things. Delete the adjective that was going to tell the reader how to feel.

Across 1,557 words the two exemplars contain exactly one stock evaluative adjective ('iconic', once). The weak pair contain 23. Specific true detail is the entertainment; adjectives are what you write when you don't have the detail.

'the smell of salt air mixing with avgas' does the work that 'a beloved and charming landmark' fails to do.

5. Put a number in every paragraph where one exists, with its date attached. Never use an intensity word in place of a count.

'Thousands of hours,' 'over a century,' 'countless generations' are unsourceable by construction — there is no document that confirms them. An exact figure is both more vivid and more defensible.

'In the first six months of 1870 alone, running day and night, the mill cut and shipped nearly 11.9 million board feet.' Note 'nearly' — the hedge is doing honest work.

6. Bound or attribute every superlative. Use 'one of the', 'among the', or a stated domain and date. If it is contested, name whose claim it is.

Charles's rule bans flat superlatives; 254 of them sit unattributed across 86 entries. Port Madison shows the fix costs nothing stylistically — it reads better hedged.

'one of the busiest industrial centers on Puget Sound' / 'the first brass and iron foundry in Washington Territory' / 'in what one account called one of the hottest political fights in county history'

7. Say plainly when the record is thin. Uncertainty is content, not weakness.

This is the sentence pattern that lets the voice stay entertaining and survive an academic sourcing audit at the same time. It converts a gap in the evidence into a moment of texture.

'The exact reasons remain unclear, perhaps rising rents, perhaps changing economics, perhaps simply the natural lifecycle of small businesses.'

8. Ban 'while' as a connective. Two facts get two sentences.

Zero uses across both exemplars; 14 in Mukilteo and 10 in Yakama. The 'X, while also Y-ing' weld is the single clearest machine signature in the corpus, and in narration it produces a clause chain the listener cannot hold.

'These technological improvements enhanced safety while reducing the isolation that had traditionally characterized lighthouse keeping.' becomes two sentences, each with a subject who did something.

9. Never let an abstract noun be the subject of a sentence. No 'the establishment of', 'the construction of', 'the introduction of', 'the automation of'.

Abstract-noun density runs 0.9 per 100 words in Port Madison and 3.9 in Bowerman against 11.9 in Mukilteo and 10.5 in Yakama. A nominalized subject hides the actor, which is exactly the sentence shape that lets an unsourced claim slip through unnoticed.

'The construction of Mukilteo Lighthouse directly supported the development of regular ferry service' → 'The lighthouse made the Whidbey ferry run possible in fog and darkness.'

10. Write section headings that could only belong to this entry, and let them carry a fact or a date.

Ten heading strings repeat across unrelated places in the corpus. A heading like 'Cultural Legacy' tells the reader nothing and proves the entry was assembled from a template.

'The Six Months It Was Slaughter County' / 'The End of an Era (January 2015)' / 'The Slow Fade'. Not: 'Educational Programs', 'Historic Preservation and Community Stewardship'.

11. Close on a fact that returns to the opening image. Do not tell the reader what it meant.

Port Madison ends with the county name it opened with and stops. Bowerman is the weaker of the two exemplars (Flesch 51 vs 70.2) largely because it ends on a stated moral — 'preserving the soul of general aviation'. Where the two exemplars disagree, follow Port Madison.

'And the county that got its name here, on a summer day in 1857, still carries it more than a century and a half later.'

12. Address the reader only for a physical experience they could actually have today, and only in an entry where you were there.

Bowerman uses 'you' four times and earns it — Charles is a pilot and the hero photo is from his own first solo cross-country. Port Madison uses it zero times. Second person from a narrator with no standing reads as tour-brochure copy.

'You can still fly to KHQM, still make the approach to Runway 24, still taxi to the parking area. But the smell of fresh coffee and bacon? That's gone.'

Never do these

  • Participial scene-setting openers with an evaluative adjective — 'Perched on a bluff overlooking the scenic waters of Possession Sound' / 'In the heart of Washington's Yakima Valley'. Six such openers in Mukilteo, one in the two exemplars combined. It delays the subject and signals brochure before the first fact arrives.
  • 'stands as' / 'serves as' / 'represents' / 'reflects' / 'provides' as the main verb. Mukilteo and Yakama use these 16 and 18 times per thousand words. They assert significance instead of showing evidence, and nothing in them can be fact-checked.
  • The 'while' weld ('X improved safety while reducing isolation'). 24 uses across the two weak entries, 0 across the exemplars. Unspeakable aloud, and it buries the second claim where no one audits it.
  • Abstract nouns as sentence subjects — 'The establishment of the ferry route represented a significant improvement in transportation reliability.' The actor vanishes, so the claim has no owner and no source.
  • Stock evaluative adjectives: beloved, charming, remarkable, crucial, iconic, profound, essential, comprehensive, spectacular, extraordinary, enduring, distinctive, sophisticated. Present 23 times in the weak pair, once in the exemplars. Each one is an unsourced editorial verdict wearing a description's clothes.
  • Metronome paragraphs. 20 of Mukilteo's 23 paragraphs are exactly two sentences; 15 of Yakama's 19 are. Exemplar paragraphs run 1 to 6 sentences with no repeating pattern. Uniformity is the most audible tell in narration.
  • Reusable section headings ('Educational Programs', 'Cultural Legacy', 'Community Integration'). They appear across unrelated entries and prove the shape came before the research.
  • Unnamed collective actors doing unquantified good — 'Local residents took pride', 'Community volunteers contributed thousands of hours', 'Community involvement created strong bonds'. Warm, unfalsifiable, unsourceable, and factually empty.
  • A closing thesis that explains the significance — 'Mukilteo Lighthouse shows the importance of community-scale maritime infrastructure and the enduring value of preserving local landmarks.' It is advocacy, it is unverifiable, and it lands last, so it is what the listener remembers.
  • Bold used for emphasis rather than for a term the reader will scan for. Bold is silent in audio, so any meaning it carries is lost in a third of the delivery surfaces. Port Madison uses none.

Mukilteo Lighthouse: Guardian of Possession Sound

Perched on a bluff overlooking the scenic waters of Possession Sound, Mukilteo Lighthouse has served as a beloved beacon and community landmark for over a century. Built in 1906 for general navigation in Possession Sound, this charming lighthouse represents the evolution of Puget Sound maritime transportation and the close relationship between lighthouse keeping and community life that characterized smaller coastal communities throughout the Pacific Northwest.

Ferry Route Development

The construction of Mukilteo Lighthouse directly supported the development of regular ferry service between the mainland and Whidbey Island, a transportation link that became essential for the economic and social development of both communities. Before reliable ferry service, residents of Whidbey Island faced difficult journeys involving multiple boat transfers and unpredictable schedules that limited their access to mainland services and markets.

The establishment of the Mukilteo-Clinton ferry route in the early 1900s represented a significant improvement in transportation reliability, allowing Whidbey Island residents to commute to mainland jobs, access medical services, and maintain family connections that had been difficult to sustain during the era of irregular boat service. The lighthouse provided the navigation aid necessary for ferries to operate safely during fog, storms, and darkness.

Ferry captains depended on Mukilteo Lighthouse's beacon to identify the proper landing approach, particularly during the frequent fog conditions that could reduce visibility to mere yards. The lighthouse's distinctive flashing pattern allowed experienced mariners to distinguish it from other lights in the busy Puget Sound traffic lanes while providing a reliable reference point for navigation in confined waters.

Community-Centered Lighthouse Design

Unlike the massive coastal lighthouses built to withstand Pacific storms, Mukilteo Lighthouse was designed as a community facility that combined navigation functions with local civic purposes. The lighthouse's modest scale and attractive architecture reflected its role serving protected inland waters rather than the open ocean, creating a structure that harmonized with the residential character of the surrounding community.

Mukilteo Lighthouse

The light station sits on Mukilteo Point at the edge of Possession Sound, on 1.46 acres. Nobody ever lived in the tower. Congress appropriated $22,000 on January 9, 1903, the Light House Service began building in 1905 and finished in eight months, and the lamp first burned on March 1, 1906 — five years before the first passenger ferry ran to Whidbey Island.

The Vessel That Nearly Went Ashore, February 21, 1902

A near miss put it here. The Secretary of the Treasury wrote to the Speaker of the House on February 21, 1902. His letter, quoted in the National Register nomination, said the tides "are very strong and deceiving at Muckilteo Point." Then came the line that bought the building: "[s]ome time ago one of the large San Francisco vessels narrowly escaped running ashore there in a fog."

The Light House Board wanted it for Everett-bound vessels and for traffic up Possession Sound and Saratoga Passage. Lumber and coastwise commerce made the case. No ferry figured in it at all. HistoryLink dates passenger service to Whidbey at 1911, car service at 1916 by tug and barge, and the Washington State Ferries takeover at 1951.

Carl Leick Framed It in Wood, 1905

Carl W. Leick (1854-1939) drew it. He had drafted for the 13th Lighthouse District since 1889, and his Grays Harbor (1898) and Admiralty Head (1903) lighthouses also survive. He framed this tower in wood at a time when most lighthouses went up in brick and concrete. His motto, per the nomination: "Build 'em stout, and make 'em last."

How tall is it? The federal record does not agree with itself. The 1977 nomination gives 33 feet above the water. The 2008 form says 35 feet on one page and a "38-foot, octagonal tower" nine pages later. Triangular squinches carry the square first floor up to an octagonal second story, and thirty-six risers climb from the watch room to the lantern house.

Every Three Hours, Christiansen Started Over

Every three hours the keeper refilled the oil lamp, trimmed the wick, adjusted the air intake in the tower, and rewound the clockwork that turned the lens. Then he oiled the compressor that drove the foghorn.

That horn was a Daboll trumpet, made by Celadon L. Daboll of New London, Connecticut. Compressed air crossed a vibrating reed. In fog its bass notes sounded every 16 seconds and could be heard up to eight miles away. Fort Casey: Sentinel of Admiralty Inlet

Stand at the edge of the gun battery and look out across Admiralty Inlet. On a clear day you can see the Olympic Peninsula to the west and the Cascade foothills to the east. What you are seeing is nearly the same view soldiers scanned anxiously for enemy warships from the 1890s through the 1940s, a century of vigilance from this windswept bluff on Whidbey Island.

The Triangle of Fire

Fort Casey was one point in a carefully calculated military strategy known as the Triangle of Fire. Military planners in the 1890s recognized that any hostile fleet threatening Puget Sound would have to pass through Admiralty Inlet, a narrow, fast-moving channel that offered a defender's dream of concentrated firepower.

Three forts were constructed to cover this passage from different angles: Casey on Whidbey Island, Worden on the Olympic Peninsula, and Flagler on Marrowstone Island. Their overlapping fields of fire would theoretically make the inlet impassable to any enemy vessel. It was sound thinking for its era, though the strategy would eventually be rendered obsolete by the emergence of air power.

The Disappearing Guns

The centerpiece of Fort Casey's armament was its battery of disappearing guns, a technology that was thoroughly practical military engineering. Each massive gun was mounted on a hydraulic counterweight carriage. When not firing, the barrel sat completely hidden below the concrete parapet, invisible to enemy rangefinders. To fire, the crew would raise the gun, take aim, pull the lanyard, and the recoil would automatically return the barrel to its hidden position below the wall.

The guns you see at Fort Casey today (two 10-inch pieces) are not the originals. The original armament was removed during World War I and II when the metal was desperately needed for the war effort. These guns were retrieved from Fort Wint in the Philippines after World War II and installed here in the 1960s for historical display.

At peak armament, Fort Casey could fire 600-pound projectiles up to eight miles into the inlet, enough range to engage a warship before it could return accurate fire at the shore batteries.

Fort Casey

Fort Casey sits on Admiralty Head, on Whidbey Island, above Admiralty Inlet. The guns are still there. Congress authorized the Secretary of War to fortify the inlet at three points on June 6, 1896, the Army bought 123 acres from Dr. John C. Kellogg for $7,200 in 1897, and by the fall of 1905 the three-fort system had cost about $7.5 million.

Two Kellogg Land Sales, 1856 and 1897

The Army was not the first federal buyer here. In August 1856 Congress appropriated $25,000 for a lighthouse at Red Bluff, and $400 of that bought 10 acres from the Kellogg family. The Army came back forty-one years later and bought 123 acres from Dr. John C. Kellogg.

Maney, Goerig and Rydstrom of Everett began work on the emplacements in August 1897. In July 1899 the Army named the post for Brigadier General Thomas Lincoln Casey, its Chief of Engineers.

Thirty-Four Guns, or Thirty-Five

Washington State Parks dates construction to July 1897 and full operation to July 19, 1902. HistoryLink tells it differently: a temporary garrison in 1901, the permanent garrison in June 1902, permanent buildings in 1903.

They also disagree by one gun. State Parks counts 35 pieces at the peak — seven 10-inch disappearing guns, six 6-inch disappearing guns, two 5-inch balanced pillar guns, four 3-inch pedestal guns, and sixteen 12-inch mortars. HistoryLink counts 34, with six 10-inch guns instead of seven and every other figure identical. Both counts are published and neither has been withdrawn. The guns sat about 100 feet above the water.

Open Your Windows: May 5, 1903

The Army test-fired the guns on May 5, 1903. At least 26 soldiers worked a single 10-inch gun, and its shell weighed 617 pounds. The Daily Herald puts the range at roughly eight miles, the National Park Service at about seven.

Denisha Blondin, quoted in the Herald, described the warning that went out to the neighbors. "They'd tell them beforehand when they were test firing to open all their windows because the reverberation would shatter the windows."

Thirteen Guns Went to Europe

In the First World War the Army dismantled thirteen of the fort's guns and shipped them to European battlefields, to be fired there rather than scrapped. In the Second it reopened as an induction and training center. In 1942 every 10-inch and 12-inch gun here was dismounted and scrapped.

The Army shut the post down for good on June 30, 1953, the same day as Forts Worden and Flagler. Park guide Mary Bloom sums up the combat record: "There was never a shot fired in anger from this fort."
